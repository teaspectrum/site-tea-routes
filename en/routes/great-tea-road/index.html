Overview

The Great Tea Road was an overland trade system connecting tea-producing regions of southern and central China with Mongolia, Siberia and European Russia. It flourished from the late seventeenth to the early twentieth century. The migrated record gives a route of about 13,000 kilometres from Wuyi Mountain in Fujian to St Petersburg, and describes a caravan journey of twelve to sixteen months one way. The route was not a single road: river, canal, cart, pack animal and sled sections were combined according to season and terrain, and the trading network was managed largely by Shanxi merchants.

History and background

Early trade and the Kyakhta gateway. The 1689 Treaty of Nerchinsk legalised Sino-Russian trade. The 1727 Treaty of Kyakhta established a border market at Kyakhta, south of Lake Baikal, and for much of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries it was the main legal gateway for Chinese tea entering Russia. Shanxi merchants such as Chang Wanda and Wang Xiangqing were among the pioneers who organised supplies from Fujian through Hankou, Shanxi and Mongolia to Kyakhta. The old record states that tea volumes grew from a few hundred dan a year at the 1727 treaty to nearly 100,000 dan a year by the mid-nineteenth century, and that at its peak tea accounted for more than 90 percent of the value exchanged at Kyakhta. The route ran roughly 13,000 kilometres and remained a going concern for about two centuries, from the late seventeenth to the early twentieth.

Hankou and the Russian tea trade. After the 1858 Treaty of Tianjin opened Hankou, Russian merchants established tea factories there. They pressed Chinese tea into bricks for long-distance transport. The three firms often named in the record — Shunfeng, Xintai and Fuchang — represented a new industrial layer in the trade. In the 1860s, Qimen black tea from Anhui became a major export, and by the 1880s Hankou was handling more than a million dan of tea a year. The city was described as the "tea port of the East", with tea chests stacked below the Jianghan Customs clock tower.

The Shanxi merchant network. Shanxi firms organised a chain that linked production, processing, transport and sale. The Chang family bought and managed tea land at Wuyi Mountain, processed tea in Hankou, transferred goods at Sheqi, kept camel teams at Guihua (modern Hohhot), and traded at Kyakhta. The old record describes this as a vertically integrated business model. Shanxi merchant houses also used remittance networks and tea certificates to settle payments across long distances. Dashengkui, one of the largest firms, is said to have owned more than 15,000 camels, employed about 7,000 people and handled an annual trade worth millions of taels of silver; such figures come from the migrated record and should be read as historical estimates rather than audited statistics.

From Wuyi to the steppe. Xiamei village at Wuyi Mountain is described as the starting point. Tea from the Wuyi area, including Zhengshan Xiaozhong and Wuyi rock tea, was gathered at Xiamei and sent by the Chongxi River and other waterways to the Yangtze. The route then ran via Hekou and Poyang Lake to Hankou. From Hankou, flat-bottomed boats went up the Han River; the more than 500 li from Hankou to Xiangyang could take nearly a month against the current. At Xiangyang (Fancheng) goods were transferred from boats to mules and carts; Sheqi in Henan was the northernmost river port and a major transshipment town; Luoyang and the Taihang passes led into Shanxi; Yanmen Pass, Datong and the Great Wall gates at Zhangjiakou and Shahukou marked the exit to the steppe.

Anhua dark tea and brick tea as money. Hunan Anhua dark tea — Tianjian, Qianliang and Qingzhuan — was pressed into bricks and packed in bamboo baskets, the most durable form for the long road. Shanxi merchants bought in Anhua, moved the tea by the Zi River and Dongting Lake to the Yangtze and Hankou, and then fed it into the main route. In Mongolia brick tea circulated as money; in Russia it was a daily necessity. Its pine-smoke and “floral fungus” aromas were, in the old record, the most familiar taste of distant China.

Guihua, the Gobi and Kyakhta. Guihua was the base for camel teams before the desert crossing. The old record describes camel "houses" of 150 to 300 animals, with guides, veterinarians, guards and cooks. Each camel carried about four chests of tea, roughly 200 kilograms. The Gobi crossing was the hardest section: summer ground temperatures could exceed 50°C, winter temperatures could fall to minus 40°C, and wells and sandstorms made the crossing dangerous. The route then reached Kulun (modern Ulaanbaatar) and continued about 300 kilometres north to Kyakhta, where Chinese and Russian merchants met at the border market. From Kyakhta, tea crossed Siberia by sled, cart and, later, rail.

Siberia and European Russia. Irkutsk, Krasnoyarsk, Novosibirsk, Tyumen, Yekaterinburg, Perm, Kazan, Nizhny Novgorod, Moscow and St Petersburg formed the Russian side of the chain. In winter, sleds carried tea across frozen rivers and snow; in summer, carts and river boats moved it west. The Trans-Siberian Railway, completed in the 1890s, cut the journey from Kyakhta to Moscow from about six months to less than two. Tea arrived in Moscow and St Petersburg both as a commodity and as a daily habit, associated with the Russian samovar. By the 1910s Russia was importing about 350,000 tonnes of Chinese tea a year, making it one of China’s largest overseas markets.

Decline and legacy. The trade weakened after the 1905 Russo-Japanese War, the 1911 revolution in China, the First World War (1914–1918) and the 1917 Russian Revolution. The new Soviet state established a tea monopoly and the Kyakhta free market closed. Indian and Ceylon tea also took market share from Chinese tea. By the 1930s the caravan route had largely ended. In 2013 China, Mongolia and Russia jointly placed the route on the transnational World Heritage tentative list; the Chinese section entered the country’s Tentative List in 2019. Heritage points include the Xiamei tea market, the Shanxi–Shaanxi guild halls at Sheqi, the Yanmen Pass road, the Zhangjiakou Great Wall gate and Kyakhta’s border remains.

Route and scope

The migrated route runs from Wuyi Mountain (Xiamei) through Hekou, Jiujiang and Hankou; up the Han River to Xiangyang; through Sheqi, Luoyang, Zezhou, Jinzhong, Taiyuan, Xinzhou, Yanmen Pass and Datong; through Zhangjiakou and Shahukou to Guihua; across the Gobi via Siziwang Banner, Saihan Tal, Erenhot, Zamyn-Uud, Sainshand and Kulun; to Kyakhta; then through Ulan-Ude, Irkutsk, Krasnoyarsk, Novosibirsk, Tyumen, Yekaterinburg, Perm, Kazan, Nizhny Novgorod, Moscow, Tver and Novgorod to St Petersburg. The shared station table records 44 nodes from Xiamei at about 200 metres to St Petersburg at about 10 metres.

The distances, durations, temperatures and caravan sizes are historical route data from the migrated record. They are not a modern navigation track, a road-condition report or a guarantee that the described route can be followed. The map and elevation profile on this page use the shared station table; missing values remain empty rather than being set to zero.

Stations

The stations below use the same node_id as the route fact table. The map and elevation profile are reading aids, not a substitute for field data.

01 · Wuyi Mountain (Xiamei Village)

Cumulative distance about 0 km; elevation about 200 m; coordinates 27.73, 117.78. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The starting point of the Great Tea Road. Xiamei village at Wuyi Mountain was the first buying stop for Shanxi merchants; the Zou family shrine, tea merchants’ houses and the old tea wharf survive. The Xiamei tea market is said to have traded several hundred dan a day, and northern Fujian tea was gathered here before leaving by the Chongxi River.

02 · Hekou Town, Jiangxi

Cumulative distance about 120 km; elevation about 80 m; coordinates 28.2, 117.75. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

An old town at the meeting of the Xin and Yanshan rivers. Wuyi tea came down the Chongxi into the Xin River here; Hekou was one of the “eight-province wharves” of the Ming and Qing, where tea changed to larger boats for Poyang Lake. Qing tea firms and guild halls survive in the town.

03 · Jiujiang

Cumulative distance about 300 km; elevation about 30 m; coordinates 29.72, 115.98. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

An important commercial port on the middle Yangtze. The first stop after tea left Poyang Lake for the Yangtze, Jiujiang’s tea market peaked in the mid-Qing, and Fujian tea was blended here. After the port opened in 1861 foreign firms set up tea warehouses, and the wharves were piled with chests.

04 · Hankou (Wuhan)

Cumulative distance about 460 km; elevation about 25 m; coordinates 30.58, 114.28. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The heart of the Great Tea Road and the great tea collecting centre of nine-province communications. After the 1858 Treaty of Tianjin opened Hankou, Russian firms built the Shunfeng, Xintai and Fuchang brick-tea factories. Hankou shipped more than 60 percent of China’s tea and was called the “tea port of the East”; chests flowed beneath the Jianghan Customs clock tower.

05 · Xiantao · Han River Route

Cumulative distance about 540 km; elevation about 25 m; coordinates 30.37, 113.45. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A river-transport hub on the lower Han. Tea changed to flat-bottomed boats at Hankou and was hauled upstream; trackers pulled the boats and covered only a few dozen li a day. The Han winds between Xiantao and Xiangyang, lined with wharves, and was one of the busiest water sections of the road.

06 · Xiangyang (Fancheng)

Cumulative distance about 680 km; elevation about 70 m; coordinates 32.02, 112.15. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A key town on the middle Han. Tea left the water here and went north by mule from the Fancheng wharf. Xiangyang was a historic battleground and the dividing point between south-bound boats and north-bound horses; tea merchants crowded both banks, and the Shanshan guild hall survives.

07 · Shedian (Sheqi), Henan

Cumulative distance about 800 km; elevation about 120 m; coordinates 33.05, 112.93. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The “first shop under heaven” on the Great Tea Road. Shedian was the northernmost wharf on the Tang River, a tributary of the Han, where boat and horse transport changed cleanly. Seventy-two streets of shops packed the old town; the Shanshan guild hall, now the Sheqi guild hall, showed Shanxi wealth. Hundreds of boats and thousands of camels and mules are said to have stopped here in a day.

08 · Luoyang

Cumulative distance about 950 km; elevation about 150 m; coordinates 34.62, 112.45. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

An ancient capital of thirteen dynasties and a central-plains hub. Tea crossed the Yellow River at the Mengjin ford; the old city keeps the Shanshan and Luze guild halls, witnesses to Shanxi tea trade. Luoyang’s peonies were famous, and tea merchants rested and saw the flowers in spring before going north.

09 · Zezhou (Jincheng)

Cumulative distance about 1,080 km; elevation about 700 m; coordinates 35.5, 112.85. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The first stop in Shanxi. The Taihang Pass, one of the eight Taihang routes, was the necessary way over the mountains. Zezhou sits at the throat between Shanxi and Henan, with steep peaks and rough roads; tea chests were carried by porters here instead of mules, and the flagstones of the Wanzicheng road still show deep cart ruts.

10 · Yuci (Jinzhong)

Cumulative distance about 1,220 km; elevation about 800 m; coordinates 37.68, 112.75. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The home of the Shanxi merchants. Yuci was the base of the Jinzhong tea firms; the Chang family, above all Chang Wanda, were core pioneers of the road. They bought tea land at Wuyi, ran the whole chain from Xiamei to Kyakhta, and built the Chang family estate at Chewand village, covering 600,000 square metres.

11 · Taiyuan

Cumulative distance about 1,280 km; elevation about 800 m; coordinates 37.87, 112.53. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The provincial capital and Shanxi merchant base. Taiyuan was the largest supply and rest stop on the northward journey. Qing-era streets were full of remittance banks, tea firms and camel teams settling silver and goods; the “tea market street” was the trade centre, and merchants hired camels and guides here.

12 · Xinzhou

Cumulative distance about 1,350 km; elevation about 800 m; coordinates 38.42, 112.73. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A commercial town of northern Shanxi. Xinzhou lay on the necessary road from Jinzhong to Yanmen Pass. The old city had a tea-tax checkpoint where all northbound tea was inspected and taxed; its livestock market was busy, and merchants bought animals here for the pass.

13 · Yanmen Pass (Daixian)

Cumulative distance about 1,430 km; elevation about 1,200 m; coordinates 39.05, 112.93. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The first pass under heaven. Yanmen was the great natural barrier of the road — “of the nine passes, Yanmen comes first.” The climb was dangerous, the road steep and covered with ice in winter, and camel teams often met blizzards. Daixian was an important post town, and a plaque in the Guandi temple records tea merchants’ prayers for safety.

14 · Datong

Cumulative distance about 1,530 km; elevation about 1,050 m; coordinates 40.08, 113.3. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The gateway of northern Shanxi and a frontier city. Datong was the last big city before the steppe, where caravans stocked grain and replaced camel gear. Shanxi guild halls and old tea firms clustered in the city, and bells rang continuously. Beyond Datong the grassland opened — the real long journey began here.

15 · Zhangjiakou (Dajingmen)

Cumulative distance about 1,680 km; elevation about 750 m; coordinates 40.82, 114.88. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The “east gate” of the Great Tea Road. Dajingmen is a famous Great Wall pass; its inscription, “great rivers and mountains,” is known across China. Zhangjiakou was the starting point of the Zhang–Kulun road, where Shanxi camel teams gathered. Outside the gate lay the Mongolian plateau — “leave Dajingmen and leave your home town” — and the caravan set out across the tea road.

16 · Shahukou (Youyu Xikou)

Cumulative distance about 1,800 km; elevation about 1,500 m; coordinates 39.97, 112.3. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The “west gate” of the Great Tea Road. Shahukou is an important Great Wall pass and the home of the “going west” folk songs. Tea merchants left China here for the Mongolian grassland. The Dashengkui firm’s camels crowded both sides of the pass — “the road through Shahukou is hard, camel bells ring for a hundred li.”

17 · Guihua (Hohhot)

Cumulative distance about 1,880 km; elevation about 1,050 m; coordinates 40.85, 111.75. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The first great town of the Mongolian grassland and the Shanxi merchants’ base in Mongolia. Guihua was headquarters of Dashengkui. Livestock trade was busy, and caravans bought camels and hired Mongolian guides. The Gobi section from Guihua to Kulun was the hardest of the whole road — “camel bells ring a thousand li, the Gobi has no end.”

18 · Siziwang Banner

Cumulative distance about 2,000 km; elevation about 1,400 m; coordinates 41.52, 111.7. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A post station deep in the Ulanqab grassland. After Guihua the caravans entered the Four Prince banner pastures, with only wind and bells across the open grass. Siziwang was the last supply point before the great Gobi — “past Siziwang, water is dearer than oil” — and the hardest water country began here.

19 · Saihantal

Cumulative distance about 2,200 km; elevation about 1,300 m; coordinates 42.7, 112. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A station in the open Gobi. “Saihantal” means “beautiful grassland” in Mongolian, but the country is already semi-desert. Caravans queued at old wells, each person limited to a kettle a day; in summer the sun burned the camels’ feet, so they travelled by night.

20 · Erenhot (China-Mongolia Border)

Cumulative distance about 2,380 km; elevation about 1,000 m; coordinates 43.65, 112. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A Gobi port on the China–Mongolia border. Camels walked on salt flats that cracked underfoot — “on the Erenhot salt road the camels’ feet bleed.” Border guards checked the goods permit, the last Qing inspection the tea chests would receive before entering Mongolia.

21 · Zamyn-Üüd

Cumulative distance about 2,420 km; elevation about 1,000 m; coordinates 43.68, 111.9. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The first stop outside China. “Zamyn-Üüd” means “gate of the road” in Mongolian. Markers were crude and caravans relied on their Mongolian guides; the Gobi offered no landmarks, and losing the way meant death.

22 · Sainshand

Cumulative distance about 2,600 km; elevation about 1,200 m; coordinates 44.88, 110.13. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A traditional post and supply station in the eastern Mongolian Gobi. Caravans rested three to five days in felt camps, for people and animals alike. “Sainshand” means “good spring”; a spring in the Gobi was worth more than gold.

23 · Ulaanbaatar (Küriye)

Cumulative distance about 2,850 km; elevation about 1,350 m; coordinates 47.92, 106.92. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The largest city and religious centre of Mongolia. Küriye was the tea transshipment hub, where caravans sold part of their load to princes and monasteries. The great monastery squares saw huge annual tea trade; Mongolians exchanged horses and furs for brick tea.

24 · Kyakhta (China-Russia Border)

Cumulative distance about 3,200 km; elevation about 760 m; coordinates 50.33, 106.45. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The most important town on the road and the hinge of the China–Russia tea trade. “Kyakhta” means “a place with tea” in Russian, and the two empires traded at this fixed border market. Chinese merchants met Russian buyers in the “trading town” on the Chinese side; millions of roubles in tea changed hands each year, and Kyakhta made dozens of Shanxi fortunes — “one Kyakhta, half the wealth of Shanxi.”

25 · Verkhneudinsk (Ulan-Ude)

Cumulative distance about 3,500 km; elevation about 530 m; coordinates 51.83, 107.6. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

An important town on the Siberian route. The first Russian city after Kyakhta, Ulan-Ude on the Selenga was a gathering place for Far Eastern tea merchants. Russian buyers took goods from Chinese merchants and repacked them here, part west to Irkutsk and part north into Siberia.

26 · Irkutsk

Cumulative distance about 3,800 km; elevation about 450 m; coordinates 52.28, 104.3. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The largest city and commercial centre of Siberia. Tea made Irkutsk rich; dozens of tea firms and teahouses filled the town, and tea warehouses lined the Angara. It was called the “Paris of Siberia,” and Russian nobles and merchants drank Chinese tea whose fragrance reached Moscow from Lake Baikal.

27 · Zima

Cumulative distance about 4,300 km; elevation about 400 m; coordinates 53.92, 102.05. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A post town in the Siberian taiga. West of Irkutsk the road crossed deep forest, muddy in summer, plagued by mosquitoes, buried in snow in winter. Zima was an important middle stop on the long forest road to Krasnoyarsk.

28 · Nizhneudinsk

Cumulative distance about 4,800 km; elevation about 350 m; coordinates 54.93, 99.02. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A post town in southern central Siberia. Nizhneudinsk on the Uda River was a supply point in the taiga; the country was thinly peopled, villages tens of kilometres apart, and Russian post-station drivers supplied fresh horses, food and lodging.

29 · Krasnoyarsk

Cumulative distance about 5,600 km; elevation about 150 m; coordinates 56.02, 92.87. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

One of Siberia’s largest tea collecting centres. A fortress city on the Yenisei, Krasnoyarsk moved tea by river; after the Trans-Siberian Railway opened at the end of the nineteenth century it became a major rail junction, and trains gradually replaced camels.

30 · Achinsk

Cumulative distance about 5,900 km; elevation about 200 m; coordinates 56.25, 90.52. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A gateway town of western Siberia. Here the continuous taiga gave way to open country. Achinsk was an important rest stop, and Russian roadside teahouses served passing Chinese merchants from samovars — “at Achinsk the forest is nearly done, the plain is ahead.”

31 · Mariinsk

Cumulative distance about 6,300 km; elevation about 150 m; coordinates 56.22, 87.75. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The last post in the western Siberian taiga. The town was named for Maria, empress of Alexander II; tea transport passed from forest to steppe here, and the climate was already milder, without Siberia’s cutting wind.

32 · Novosibirsk

Cumulative distance about 7,000 km; elevation about 150 m; coordinates 55.03, 82.92. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A new city on the West Siberian plain. Novosibirsk grew quickly at the end of the nineteenth century with the Trans-Siberian Railway and Ob River shipping; tea was transshipped here in bulk, and from the early twentieth century its market linked the Urals and the Far East.

33 · Barabinsk

Cumulative distance about 7,500 km; elevation about 120 m; coordinates 53.73, 77.77. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A post station on the Baraba steppe. The endless black-earth grassland was very different from the Mongolian Gobi; merchants changed to horse-drawn carts at Russian post stations, far faster than camels. From here the road took on a European pace.

34 · Omsk

Cumulative distance about 8,100 km; elevation about 80 m; coordinates 54.98, 73.37. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

An old Siberian fortress on the Irtysh. Omsk was one of the oldest Russian cities in Siberia and the last Siberian gate before Europe. Merchants sent part of the tea north by the Irtysh to Tobolsk and the far north.

35 · Ishim

Cumulative distance about 8,600 km; elevation about 80 m; coordinates 56.12, 69.42. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

An old commercial post in the southern West Siberian plain. The Ishim steppe was wide and open; carts raced along the great road toward the Urals. Villages grew closer and Russian farmers welcomed the tea merchants with bread and kvass.

36 · Tyumen

Cumulative distance about 9,100 km; elevation about 80 m; coordinates 57.15, 65.53. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

One of the earliest Siberian cities. Tyumen is the “gate of Siberia” and the last great stop before the Urals. Merchants rested here — “cross the Urals and Europe begins.” The town was known for furs and tea, and Russian and Central Asian traders crowded its market.

37 · Yekaterinburg

Cumulative distance about 9,600 km; elevation about 250 m; coordinates 56.84, 60.6. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A mining and commercial centre at the foot of the Urals. Yekaterinburg marks the geographical passage from Asia to Europe — “the tea city on the Europe–Asia line.” Tea arrived from the Asian side and crossed the Urals into European Russia; merchants celebrated the safe passage of the hardest road, with only 4,000 kilometres of European plain ahead.

38 · Perm

Cumulative distance about 10,100 km; elevation about 150 m; coordinates 58, 56.25. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A city on the western slopes of the Urals, on the Kama River. Tea came by road from Yekaterinburg and changed to river boats at Perm for the Volga system. The tea wharves were busy: water transport from here to Moscow cost far less than land carriage.

39 · Kazan

Cumulative distance about 10,600 km; elevation about 60 m; coordinates 55.8, 49.1. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The capital of Tatarstan on the Volga. Kazan was a Volga tea-trade hub, with tea from Asia moving north and south along the river. Tatar merchants were important brokers in Russia, at home in Russian, Chinese and Turkic languages — natural interpreters on the road.

40 · Nizhny Novgorod

Cumulative distance about 11,200 km; elevation about 120 m; coordinates 56.3, 44. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

An old Russian capital and great trading town at the confluence of the Volga and Oka. The annual Nizhny Novgorod fair was the largest in Russia, and Chinese tea was wholesaled here across the empire. Chinese merchants showed Wuyi black tea and Anhua dark tea at the fair — “the tea fragrance of Russia comes from the Nizhny Novgorod fair.”

41 · Moscow

Cumulative distance about 11,700 km; elevation about 150 m; coordinates 55.75, 37.62. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The imperial capital. Moscow was the European core of the Great Tea Road; the tea markets near the Kremlin were full of fragrance. Samovars burned day and night in noble drawing rooms, and Chinese black tea became part of upper-class daily life — “the samovar song of Moscow is the last echo of the Great Tea Road.”

42 · Tver

Cumulative distance about 12,000 km; elevation about 140 m; coordinates 56.85, 35.9. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

An old capital between Moscow and St Petersburg. Tea on the way to St Petersburg had to pass Tver; this Upper Volga town saw tea move from a noble luxury to a drink of the whole Russian people.

43 · Veliky Novgorod

Cumulative distance about 12,400 km; elevation about 30 m; coordinates 58.55, 31.28. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

One of Russia’s oldest cities. Veliky Novgorod was a key point on the old route “from the Varangians to the Greeks”; centuries later Chinese tea followed the same water and land roads to the town. It was the last transshipment stop before St Petersburg.

44 · Saint Petersburg

Cumulative distance about 13,000 km; elevation about 10 m; coordinates 59.93, 30.32. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The terminus of the Great Tea Road. The imperial capital and “Venice of the North” on the Baltic. From Xiamei under Wuyi Mountain, tea had travelled 13,000 kilometres to this end. In the Winter Palace and on noble tea tables, a cup of Chinese black tea from the other side of Eurasia carried the soils and history of the whole journey. Here the road was complete.

Trade teas

The old record lists Zhengshan Xiaozhong, Qimen black tea, Anhua dark tea (Tianjian and Qianliang), Wuyi rock tea, Lu'an tea, Hubei green brick tea and Fujian oolong. The most important export categories were black tea and compressed brick tea. Brick tea was dense, resistant to moisture and well suited to camel, cart and sled transport; it was also used in Mongolia as a form of currency and as an everyday drink. Anhua dark tea travelled by the Zi River and Dongting Lake to the Yangtze and then to Hankou, where it joined the main route. A trade association does not mean that every stop produced every listed tea.

Visiting note

The route can be explored through museums and surviving commercial architecture in Wuyi, Hankou, Sheqi, Shanxi, Zhangjiakou, Hohhot and along the Russian tea road. Zhengshan Xiaozhong

Zhengshan Xiaozhong is a small-leaf black tea from Tongmu Pass, Wuyishan, Fujian, traditionally pine-smoked and known for pine smoke and longan sweetness. It is one of China’s earliest black teas to reach Europe, but “all black tea originated here” is an overstatement.

China · Anhui

Lu'an Guapian

Lu'an Guapian is a flat, melon-seed-shaped green tea from the Dabie Mountains of Anhui, and a geographical-indication product. It is unusual among famous Chinese greens for using a single mature leaf with no bud and no stem. The leaf is pan-fixed, shaped, baked and finished with repeated charcoal “old-fire” passes, giving a chestnut or floral aroma and a thick, sweet cup.
