The Shu Road Tea Route

China · The Shu Road Tea Route (Jinniu Road)

The Shu Road Tea Route runs from Hanzhong · Ancient Nanzheng to Chengdu · Jinguan City, with 17 stations. Read the endpoints and station table first, then ask which producing areas, markets and transport links it connected; it is not modern navigation.

Overview

The Shu Road is the collective name for several mountain routes that linked the Guanzhong plain, where Xi'an stands, with the Sichuan Basin and Chengdu. The Jinniu Road, the Baoxie Road, the Micang Road and the Lychee Road all belonged to this wider system. This article follows the Jinniu Road as a reading sample: from the Hanzhong Basin south through Mianxian, Ningqiang, Chaotian, Guangyuan and Zhaohua, past Jianmenguan and Zitong, then through Mianyang, Deyang and Guanghan to Chengdu. The shared station table records seventeen nodes and a cumulative distance of about 580 kilometres. The old Chinese page gives about 600 kilometres; the two figures use different start and end points, so both are kept and neither is forced into the other.

The corridor carried official grain, salt, cloth, horses and tea. Some Sichuan tea—Mengding tea from the edge of the Chengdu Plain, Ya'an dark tea from the western foothills, and various border teas—travelled north through Guangyuan and Hanzhong into the northwest or the Central Plains. Tea was not the only cargo, and it may not have been the largest by weight. Calling the whole Shu Road "China's first national tea road" is a later rhetorical flourish; this page does not use it.

History and background

A network, not a single road

The Qinling and Daba ranges separate the Sichuan Basin from the Guanzhong plain. No ancient engineer could lay one continuous flat road across both ranges. Instead, builders used river valleys, ridges, passes and post stations to create several routes that could substitute for one another when a section was washed out or closed by war. The Jinniu Road is the best known of these, also called the Stone Ox Road after the legend of the stone ox that dropped gold.

Different dynasties repaired, abandoned and rerouted the roads. Modern national highways, railways and expressways now lie on top of the old alignments. "Shu Road" is therefore better understood as a transport network than as a single trail that a traveller can follow from one end to the other.

The stone ox legend is not an engineering record

The best-known story says that King Hui of Qin released stone oxen that appeared to drop gold. The King of Shu, greedy for the gold, sent five strong men to open a mountain path to bring the oxen in; Qin's army then followed that path and conquered Shu. The story explains the name Jinniu Road and turns a long political and military process into a single dramatic episode.

Qin's conquest of Shu took place in the late Warring States period, but a road does not come into existence because of one legend. It is more plausible that routes between Qin and Shu already existed and that the campaign used existing paths, guides and supply lines. This page treats the stone ox as cultural narrative, not as a construction diary.

How tea moved

Mengshan tea craft was added to China's national intangible cultural heritage list in 2021, with Mengding Ganlu as one of its representative products. Local gazetteers and official introductions state that Mengshan tea entered imperial tribute in the Tang dynasty and continued as tribute into the Qing. Tribute tea travelled through the post-station system; commercial tea travelled through merchant networks. They might use the same road, but the organisations, inspections and transport charges were different.

Ya'an dark tea, also known as Nanlu border tea, is another thread. Its traditional craft was listed as national intangible heritage in 2008. The Ya'an cultural and tourism bureau notes that in the Qing dynasty Sichuan supplied a large share of the tea entering Tibet, with Ya'an border tea as one of the main sources. Border tea left Ya'an, Tianquan and Yingjing, passed through Kangding and Ganzi and entered the Tibetan plateau. It overlaps with the Shu Road, but its main direction is westward, not northward. Describing Ya'an dark tea as tea that went north along the Shu Road would erase the separate identity of the Sichuan-Tibet tea-horse route.

The most accurate summary is therefore: the Shu Road tea route is one of the corridors through which Sichuan tea left the basin and reached the northwest and the Central Plains. Mengding tea represents the tribute and famous-tea tradition; Ya'an dark tea represents the border-tea and Tibetan trade. They may meet at Chengdu, Guangyuan or Hanzhong, but they did not travel in the same direction.

Passes, plank roads and ancient cypresses

Jianmenguan stands at a gap in the Dajian Mountains and is one of the most famous defiles on the Jinniu Road. The gate tower visible today is a modern reconstruction; visitors need to distinguish the reconstructed scenic architecture from the surviving road and pass remains. The Sichuan provincial government's protection document states that the Jianmen Shu Road refers mainly to the Jinniu Road within Guangyuan, with ancient roads, post stations, passes, cypresses and cliff carvings along the corridor.

Mingyue Gorge preserves plank-road holes and road remains from several periods and is an important part of the Jianmen Shu Road heritage. Builders cut holes into the cliff, inserted timber beams and laid planks. The resulting walkway was narrow—usually enough for one or two people, or a pack animal, to pass in single file. It was not a five-hundred-metre-wide avenue. The holes visible today span more than one era; their dating requires archaeological reports, not a glance from a tourist path.

The Cuiyun Corridor is known for its ancient cypress trees. The age, planting date and number of surviving trees should be taken from forestry and heritage-authority surveys. Online claims such as "2,300 years old, planted by King Hui of Qin" or "one hundred thousand trees" usually mix poetry, legend and census figures. This page does not fix those numbers.

From post road to highway and railway

In the 1930s, the Sichuan-Shaanxi Highway was built on the base of the old post road and became an important link between Sichuan and northern China during the war with Japan. In the 1950s the Baocheng Railway opened, taking trains through the Qinling and ending the ancient road's role in bulk freight. Expressways and the Xi'an-Chengdu high-speed railway later cut the journey to a few hours. A journey along the Shu Road today passes through a layered landscape of ancient road remains, scenic roads, national highways and high-speed rail.

Route and scope

This page uses the Jinniu Road reading sample: Hanzhong (ancient Nanzheng) → Mianxian (Wuhou Shrine) → Da'an (Jinniu Post) → Ningqiang (Jinniu Gorge) → Qipanguan → Wudingguan → Chaotian (Mingyue Gorge) → Guangyuan (Lizhou) → Zhaohua (Jiameng) → Jianmenguan → Jiange → Zitong (Qiqushan) → Mianyang (Fucheng) → Luojiang → Deyang → Guanghan → Chengdu. The station table records about 580 kilometres and elevations from roughly 400 to 1,500 metres.

A historical caravan would have taken weeks, not days; the old page gives twenty to thirty days. The actual time depended on cargo weight, season, inspections at passes and available fodder. By national highway or high-speed rail today the same direction takes hours or a day. These are not the same kind of journey, and the modern time should not be used to argue that the ancient route was easy.

Stations

The stations below use the same node_id as the route fact table. The map and elevation profile are reading aids, not a substitute for field data.

01 · Hanzhong · Ancient Nanzheng

Hanzhong is the transport hub of the basin, where the Jinniu Road and other Shu Road branches converge or transfer goods. Ancient Nanzheng was a regional administrative and commercial centre; tea, salt, cloth and official grain all passed through it.

02 · Mianxian · Wuhou Shrine

The Wuhou Shrine at Mianxian commemorates Zhuge Liang. Local storytelling often links merchants' prayers before departure to the shrine, but continuous documentary evidence for a specific ritual is lacking.

03 · Da'an · Jinniu Post

Jinniu Post was one station in the Jinniu Road's post system. The stone-ox legend explains the road's name; it should not be read as a construction record.

04 · Ningqiang · Jinniu Gorge

The Jinniu Gorge area is high and deeply cut, with the old road following the stream valley. Most surviving road surfaces were repaired in later periods and cannot be used to reconstruct the Warring States or Qin-Han original.

05 · Qipanguan · Shaanxi-Sichuan Border

Qipanguan was an old pass on the Shaanxi-Sichuan border. The road switchbacks up the mountain, and the pass long served inspection and transshipment.

06 · Wudingguan · Highest Point of the Jinniu Road

Wudingguan is the highest node in the shared table. The story of the Five Strong Men opening the mountain belongs to legend and is often mixed with the historical narrative of Qin's conquest of Shu.

07 · Chaotian · Mingyue Gorge Plank Road

Mingyue Gorge preserves plank-road holes and road remains from several periods and is an important part of the Jianmen Shu Road heritage. The walkway was narrow, usually passable only in single file by people or pack animals.

08 · Guangyuan · Lizhou

Guangyuan was called Lizhou in earlier periods, where the Jialing River and the overland road met. Local gazetteers and modern studies both stress its role as a transfer point in northern Sichuan.

09 · Zhaohua · Jiameng Pass

Zhaohua was known as Jiameng and is an ancient city and pass on the Shu Road. The online claim that "Jiameng means tea" has no reliable documentary support, and this page does not use it.

10 · Jianmenguan · Tianxia Xiongguan

Jianmenguan stands at the Dajian Mountain gap and is one of the most dramatic defiles on the Jinniu Road. The gate tower is a later reconstruction; the scenic architecture and the surviving road remains should be read separately.

11 · Jiange · Jianmen Ancient Town

Jianmen Ancient Town was a supply point south of the pass. Jianmen tofu is a local food; its later association with a caravan "victory feast" is storytelling rather than documented practice.

12 · Zitong · Qiqushan Temple

Qiqushan Temple is an important shrine of Wenchang devotion. The old road passed in front of it, but the specific prayers of merchants and travellers are not systematically recorded.

13 · Mianyang · Fucheng

Mianyang sits at the northern edge of the Chengdu Plain, where Fujiang River transport met the overland route. The old page's claim that it was the "largest tea wharf in northern Sichuan in the Qing dynasty" lacks a verifiable figure; this page keeps only its role as a transfer node.

14 · Luojiang · Tiaoyuan Ancient Town

Luojiang is an ancient town on the northern rim of the Chengdu Plain, alongside local cultural sites such as the former residence of Li Tiaoyuan. The condition of the old road should be taken from cultural-heritage authority publications.

15 · Deyang · Jingcheng

Deyang is one of the northern gateways to the Chengdu Plain. The old page's explanations of place names such as "Tea Market Street" and "Tea Ox Street" lack independent evidence, so they are not expanded here.

16 · Guanghan · Sanxingdui

Guanghan is the node before Chengdu. The idea that Sanxingdui bronzes held Shu tea is speculation without reliable testing evidence; this page does not present "bronze vessels filled with tea" as fact.

17 · Chengdu · Jinguan City

Chengdu is the terminus and the consumer market of the plain. Mengding tea, Ya'an dark tea and other Sichuan teas gathered here before entering the city's teahouses or moving outward through other networks.

Trade teas

  • Mengding Ganlu: one of the representative Mengshan teas, with a traditional craft listed as national intangible heritage. The tribute narrative is supported by official introductions, but specific tribute quantities and dates require the original tribute archives.
  • Ya'an dark tea (Nanlu border tea): listed as national intangible heritage in 2008 and sold mainly to Kham and Tibet. In the Qing dynasty Sichuan supplied a large share of the tea entering Tibet; Ya'an border tea was one of the main sources.
  • Sichuan border tea, Qingcheng Xueya, Pingshan fried green tea: teas recorded by the old page. Their connection to the Shu Road is that they may have used the same transfer network, not that all of them travelled north on the Jinniu Road.

Visiting notes

This page offers a historical-geographical reading, not a hiking guide, permit or modern safety guarantee. Jianmenguan, Mingyue Gorge, Zhaohua and the Cuiyun Corridor are scenic areas or protected heritage sites; opening hours, tickets and drone rules are set by local authorities. The old road remains are fragile. Do not climb the surviving plank holes or take rubbings from the carvings.
