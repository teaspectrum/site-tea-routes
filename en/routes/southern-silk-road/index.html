Overview

The Southern Silk Road is a multi-era network that runs from the Chengdu plain through southern Sichuan and western Yunnan into northern Myanmar, then over the Indo–Myanmar border mountains into northeast India. It is not an ancient proper name, and it was never a single highway maintained from end to end by one government. This article uses 32 stations as a reading line: Chengdu, Ya'an, Xichang, Dali, Baoshan, Tengchong, Bhamo, Myitkyina, the Pangsau Pass, Ledo, Tinsukia, Dibrugarh, Sivasagar, Jorhat and Guwahati. The station table totals about 2,850 km and ranges from about 55 to 2,200 m in elevation. These are rounded historical scales, not survey results. The old page claimed about 3,200 km; its intervals and geographical order did not agree, so this article does not retain that figure.

The name needs a caveat. No ancient text uses 'Southern Silk Road' as a fixed proper name. In the 1980s, Sichuan and Yunnan scholars adopted the term for the overland trade routes that linked southwest China with foreign lands. Older histories more often use 'Shu-Shendu Road' for the western line from Chengdu through Yunnan to India. The two overlap, but they are not coextensive. The Southern Silk Road also includes the northern Qin–Shu roads, the southern Butou and Jinsang routes, and the eastern Zangke or Yelang route. This page takes the western line as its main sample; it does not turn the whole network into one line.

'Tea route' needs a second caveat. The goods Zhang Qian saw at Daxia were Shu cloth and Qiong bamboo staffs, not tea. In the pre-Han and Han periods the Shu-Shendu corridor was primarily a trade network for textiles, bamboo and wooden goods, salt, metal vessels and precious materials. Tea became a bulk commodity on overlapping routes only after the Tang–Song period; Pu'er, Ya'an border tea and Xiaguan tuocha belong to the Ming–Qing and modern periods. Calling the whole Southern Silk Road a 'tea route' is a present-day thematic label made from the perspective of tea culture, not a name used by ancient caravans.

History and context

A modern name over several ancient networks

The National Ethnic Affairs Commission's 'Da Zhonghua' interview with Professor Ba Shengchao of Kunming University of Science and Technology divides the Southern Silk Road into four networks. The northern line is based on the Qin–Shu roads between the Guanzhong plain and Chengdu. The western line is based on the Shu-Shendu road and the Sichuan–Tibet–India routes linking Sichuan, Yunnan and Tibet with Myanmar and India. The southern line runs from Sichuan through Yunnan to Vietnam and the Indochina peninsula, and is known in historical texts as the Butou and Jinsang roads. The eastern line runs from Sichuan through Guizhou, Guangxi and Guangdong to the South China Sea, and is called the Zangke or Yelang road. This four-network frame is the first reason to speak of a network rather than a road.

The western line is itself segmented. Ba Shengchao lists the Linqiong, XiYi, Maoniu, Lingguan, Minjiang, Bo or Wuchi, Bonan and Yongchang roads. From Xichang, the Lingguan road forks: one branch goes through Yanyuan, Ninglang, Yongsheng, Lijiang, Jianchuan and Eryuan to Dali; another goes through Dechang, Miyi, Huili, Panzhihua, Yongren, Dayao, Nanhua, Xiangyun and Midu to Dali. From Dali, the Bonan and Yongchang roads continue west. Drawing these segments as one continuous line makes the page readable, but real caravans chose branches according to season, passes, taxes, goods and local relations.

The Tea Horse Road is a separate layer of nomenclature. The Baoshan culture and tourism bureau notes that the term was proposed in the 1990s by scholars including Mu Jihong and Chen Baoya and later gained wide acceptance. Baoshan's old roads are both part of the Southern Silk Road–Yongchang Road and part of the seventh batch of national key cultural relics designated as the Tea Horse Road. The two concepts overlap, but they are not the same: the Tea Horse Road centres on tea–horse markets and caravan transport, while the Southern Silk Road is broader in space and time.

Zhang Qian's report: the evidence stops at cloth and bamboo staffs

The Shiji's 'Treatise on Dayuan' records that Zhang Qian, while at Daxia, saw Qiong bamboo staffs and Shu cloth. He asked where they came from and was told that Daxia merchants bought them in Shendu. Shendu lay several thousand li southeast of Daxia. Zhang Qian concluded that Shendu could not be far from Shu and that a route through Shu would be more direct than the routes through the Qiang country or the Xiongnu lands. Emperor Wu accepted the argument and sent envoys from Shu and Jianwei in four directions. The northern envoys were blocked by the Di and Zuo, the southern by the Sui and Kunming; some envoys were killed or robbed, and the mission never reached Shendu or Daxia.

The passage proves two things. First, before Zhang Qian's report, Sichuan goods had already reached India through some southwestern route and were then re-exported by Indian merchants to Daxia. Second, the Han court tried to bring this private trade under official control, and its first attempt failed. The passage does not prove that a tea route existed in the fourth century BCE, and it does not prove that Mengding tea was the route's most valuable Tang cargo. The Shiji's list of Shu goods is cloth and bamboo staffs; tea is absent.

Some scholars infer from glass beads found in a Warring States Chu tomb that the Southern Silk Road was open by the fourth century BCE, and some estimate that it preceded the northern Silk Road by 200–300 years. These are research judgements, not dated opening records. The beads may have come from India, or may have passed through several intermediaries. The step from 'possible' to 'earliest tea road' is too wide. This article retains the scholarly possibility of a route by the fourth century BCE, but does not treat it as the beginning of tea trade.

The Mengding legend needs the same downgrade. The story that Wu Lizhen planted seven tea trees in 53 BCE is an important local memory on Mengding Mountain, but the surviving texts are much later and cannot serve as a Western Han planting record. Mengding tea became famous and was sent as tribute in the Tang; that is a fact in tea-cultural history. The further claim that it was 'the most precious cargo on the Shu-Shendu road' has no ledger, waybill or transport record behind it.

The Wuchi, Lingguan and Yongchang roads

The Wuchi, or Five-Foot, Road usually refers to the route from Bo Dao near present-day Yibin southwest through Yanjin, Zhaotong and Qujing, then toward Kunming, Chuxiong and Dali. The Shiji's 'Treatise on the Southwest Barbarians' says that in Qin times Chang E opened the Wuchi Road and that officials were stationed in the countries along it. The road was named for its width of about five chi. The exact date of its first construction, its engineering extent and the relative roles of King Zhao of Qin and the First Emperor are described differently in different local sources. What is clear is that by the Qin and Han periods it was a major route for imperial expansion into Yunnan.

The Lingguan Road usually refers to the route from Chengdu through Linqiong, Ya'an, Yingjing, Hanyuan, Yuexi, Xichang, Huili and Panzhihua into Dayao and Dali. During the reign of Emperor Wu, Sima Xiangru was sent to open roads in the Xi Yi lands; the route took its name from the Lingguan pass in Yuexi. The Wuchi and Lingguan roads were not unrelated lines. They met near Dali and continued west as the Yongchang Road.

'Yongchang Road' has a narrow and a broad sense. In the narrow sense it is the Baoshan–Tengchong–Myanmar section; in the broad sense it covers the whole westward route from Dali through Yongping and Baoshan to Tengchong. The Baoshan municipal government's article says that after the Wuchi and Lingguan roads met at Dali, travellers 'first crossed Bonan Mountain and the Lancang River', passed Jihong Bridge into Baoshan, then went southwest through Pingpo and Shuizhai, over the Luomin Mountains to Guangpo, and through Jinji and Banqiao to the old city of Baoshan. From Baoshan to Tengchong there were three routes: a northern route through Wafang and Wenshang to Menggu Ferry and the north Zhaigongfang of the Gaoligong Mountains; a middle route through Qinggangba, Shuanghong Bridge and the south Zhaigongfang; and a southern route through Pupiao, Huiren Bridge, Bawan and Mopanshi. All three led toward Tengchong, but through different passes, valleys and post stations.

Tea enters the network after the Tang–Song period

Tea was not the leading cargo of the pre-Han Shu-Shendu road, but it did later enter the same geographical corridor. During the Tang and Song, tea–horse markets developed between Sichuan, Yunnan and Tibet. Ya'an border tea travelled north along the Sichuan–Tibet Tea Horse Road into Kham and Tibet, while Pu'er tea travelled north along the Yunnan–Tibet route and south into Southeast Asia. These tea routes overlapped with parts of the Southern Silk Road around Ya'an, Dali and Baoshan, but their markets, institutions and directions were different.

Ya'an's chronology is comparatively clear. The Yucheng district government's article says that Ya'an is the starting point of the Sichuan–Tibet Tea Horse Road and a junction of the Southern Silk Road and the Tea Horse Road; the Song dynasty applied the tea–horse law; in the Qianlong period the counties of Ya'an, Tianquan and Yingjing were assigned coarse border tea for the Kham–Tibet market, called Nanbian route tea. 'Nanbian route tea' is not a single tea name but a category of border tea; its processing involved 32 steps and was designed for long transport and storage. It went mainly north to Kangding and the Tibetan lands, not west along the Shu-Shendu road into Myanmar.

Pu'er's export geography was more complex. The Yunnan local-gazetteer office's overview says that from the Qing to the Republican period Pu'er was sold to Tibet and also shipped through Myanmar and India to Tibet, or south to Siam and Vietnam. It gives a summary figure of 30,000 dan sold to Tibet and says that in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries more than 50,000 horses a year entered the tea areas between Xishuangbanna, Simao and Pu'er. In the 1930s and 1940s much Yunnan tea still travelled through Myanmar and India to Tibet. A distinction is necessary here: Tibet was the market, while Burma and India were transit routes. The same tea could go south out of Yunnan and then turn north to Tibet, or it could take the direct Yunnan–Tibet road.

These figures are summary statistics from a local gazetteer, without annual or customs-house breakdowns. They show that tea had become an important Yunnan export in the late Qing and Republican periods; they cannot be back-projected into a Han-era mass trade. Shou Pu'er's wo dui fermentation was developed in the later twentieth century. Treating shou Pu'er as an ancient caravan product is another chronological error.

Bridges, post stations and the Tengchong border market

West of Dali, caravans followed the Yangbi River into the western slope of the Cangshan range, crossed Bonan Mountain and reached the Lancang. The Bonan Road in Yongping takes its name from Bonan Mountain and is one section of the Southern Silk Road. It was later included in the seventh batch of national key cultural relics designated as the Tea Horse Road. Its heritage value lies in the combination of stone paving, post stations, bridge sites and roadside villages, not in a single ancient poem.

Lanjin Ferry and Jihong Bridge were key crossings of the Lancang on the Yongchang Road. The Longyang culture and tourism bureau says Jihong was called the 'first bridge of the southwest' and was a major node of the Southern Silk Road. It was completely destroyed by the Xiaowan hydropower project. The Putuo cliff west of the bridge holds 40 carved inscriptions, 30 of them legible, including 'First Bridge of the Southwest', 'Lanjin Ferry', 'Key to the South' and 'Cliff-side Marvel'. The bridge site was rebuilt many times; the present structure is not a Han original. Any claim that it is 'China's oldest iron-chain bridge' must first state what is being counted and what survives.

The Nu River was the next gate. The Huiren Bridge site in Daojie village, Lujiang township, Longyang district, about 2 km west of the village on the Nu River, is a national key cultural relic within the Tea Horse Road heritage. The bridge was first built in the Qing, rebuilt several times, and long served the southern Baoshan–Tengchong route across the Nu. Shuanghong Bridge was an important middle-route crossing between Baoshan and Tengchong: 162.5 m long, converted to an iron-chain bridge in 1789, and one of the better-preserved old bridges on the route. The two bridges belonged to different routes and cannot be merged into a single 'only Nu River crossing'.

Tengchong, anciently Tengyue, was one of the most important border emporia in western Yunnan. Heshun is a border emigrant hometown on the Southern Silk Road; for centuries Heshun people travelled abroad in the 'zou yifang' movement, creating transnational firms, caravan culture and jade culture. Tengchong's trade included jade, cotton, tea, salt, ironware and daily goods. Tea was one category among several. The old page's claim that Tengyue was 'the largest tea market in southwest China' and handled 'tens of thousands of dan a year' gives no year, unit or statistical source, and is not retained.

Myanmar and northeast India: river ports, passes and roads

Beyond Tengchong, Lianghe and Yingjiang, Bhamo is a river port and trading town on the upper Irrawaddy, and one of the first stops for Yunnan caravans entering Myanmar. From Bhamo one could go south down the Irrawaddy to Mandalay and Yangon, or north to Myitkyina. The old page linked Bhamo, Mandalay and Myitkyina into one south-to-north-then-west line; geographically that involves a detour. This article treats Mandalay as a southern branch, not part of the main chain.

Myitkyina is the capital of Kachin State and a transport hub on the upper Irrawaddy. The river and road between Myitkyina and Bhamo served different functions in different periods, and the twentieth-century Stilwell Road made Myitkyina a base for the Allied reconquest of Burma. Calling Myitkyina 'the Pu'er hub of Myanmar' exaggerates the weight of one commodity; it was a node for many goods, soldiers, refugees and officials.

The Pangsau Pass crosses the Patkai range on the Indo–Myanmar border and is one of the passes from Myanmar into northeast India. Its elevation is about 1,100 m. In tropical rainforest that is not high, but the track was rough, muddy in the monsoon and malarial; both caravans and armies found it difficult. During the Second World War the Stilwell Road ran from Ledo through the Pangsau Pass into Burma. The road made the pass a military transport node, but it was not the only ancient gateway. Other passes, river valleys and sea routes also connected the two sides.

Ledo, in the extreme northeast of Assam, was the starting point of the Stilwell Road. In 1942 China, the United States and Britain decided to build a road from Ledo through Myitkyina to Kunming; Ledo became a wartime logistics base. Its reputation comes from the road and the war, not from tea planting. There is also no reliable record that ancient caravans treated Ledo as a fixed station. This article treats Ledo as a twentieth-century road node while acknowledging that it lies at the western end of an older cross-border corridor.

Assam: separating migration from the tea industry

Assam has two histories that must be kept apart. The first is the political history of the Ahom kingdom. The Indian Press Information Bureau's research brief says that Tai-Ahom migrants came from China and established capitals in the Brahmaputra valley between the twelfth and eighteenth centuries; Choraideo became the first capital and royal burial ground under Chaulung Siu-ka-pha, and the burial tradition lasted about 600 years. This migration is one of the most important human links between Assam and Yunnan, but it concerns a political community and its language and culture, not tea seeds or tea-drinking customs.

The second is the commercial tea industry. The Indian Tea Board describes Assam as one of the world's largest tea-growing regions; Tocklai is the oldest and largest research station of its kind; Assam produces both orthodox and CTC tea, and Assam orthodox has a registered geographical indication. The industry was created in the nineteenth century by colonial capital, local wild tea resources, introduced planting material and new processing technology. It cannot be reduced to 'the Yunnan tea road brought tea trees directly to Assam'.

The Chinese foreign-ministry article on Assam provides a useful counterpoint. Local hosts say their ancestors migrated from southwest China in the early thirteenth century and founded the Ahom kingdom; the same article notes that Assam tea was introduced from Fujian in the nineteenth century. The two statements separate human migration from tea introduction. Both may be true, but neither can substitute for the other, and neither supports the claim that Assam's tea trees came from Yunnan two thousand years ago.

The old page also called Assam black tea and Pu'er 'two leaves on one tree'. Both belong to Camellia sinensis, but their varieties, processing and industrial histories differ. Sharing a species does not prove a direct transmission route. Assam has its own large-leaf tea resources, and nineteenth-century plantations introduced planting material from several sources. The species relationship is not evidence of a single origin line.

The twentieth century: a road layer atop an old corridor

During the Second World War, China, the United States and Britain built the China–India Road, also called the Stilwell Road. The Chinese party-history site 'Qiyi Wang' says construction began in 1942; on 27 January 1945 Chinese forces from India and China met near Mangyou, and the road was completed. The route ran from Ledo in India through Myitkyina in Burma to Kunming in Yunnan, was 1,736 km long, and delivered more than 50,000 tonnes of supplies to China's war effort.

The road ran through the same geographical corridor as the old Yunnan–Burma trade, but it belonged to a different era. Caravans used mountain tracks, ferries and bridges; the twentieth-century road used bulldozers, Bailey bridges, trucks and airfields. Caravans carried tea, salt and cotton; wartime convoys carried petrol, ammunition, medical supplies and troops. Calling the Stilwell Road a 'continuation of the thousand-year tea road' erases the differences in technology, politics and war. A more accurate description is that the two layers sit atop one another in the same corridor, each belonging to its own period.

Route and scope

The 32 stations form an east-to-west, then north-to-southwest reading line:

Chengdu (Jinguan City) → Shuangliu (Wanli Bridge) → Xinjin (Wujin Ferry) → Ya'an (Rain City) → Yingjing (Yandao) → Hanyuan (Qingxi Pass) → Yuexi (Lingguan Road) → Xichang (Qiongdu) → Dechang (Phoenix Town) → Huili (Old Town) → Panzhihua (Lazha Ferry) → Yongren (Fangshan) → Dayao (Shiyang Old Town) → Dali (Yeyu) → Yangbi (Shimen Pass) → Yongping (Bonan Mountain) → Lanjin Ferry/Jihong Bridge → Baoshan (Yongchang) → Pupiao (Ancient Post Station) → Nu River/Huiren Bridge → Tengchong (Tengyue) → Lianghe (Nandian) → Yingjiang (Tongbi Pass) → Bhamo → Myitkyina → Pangsau Pass → Ledo → Tinsukia → Dibrugarh → Sivasagar → Jorhat → Guwahati.

This is not the only route. From Yunnan one could also leave through Baoshan–Longling–Mangshi–Wanding–Ruili to Mandalay, or from Tengchong directly north to Myitkyina. From Tengchong to Bhamo there were several mountain routes. From Myanmar to India one could go through Manipur or Mizoram, or take a sea route. The Tengchong–Lianghe–Yingjiang–Bhamo–Myitkyina–Pangsau line is singled out because it recurs in the Southern Silk Road, the southern Tea Horse Road, modern Yunnan–Burma trade and the Stilwell Road narratives, and because the sources are relatively concentrated. It is a reading sample, not a claim that every caravan took this exact path.

The station table makes three structural corrections. First, it removes Xidong. The old table placed Xidong between Yingjiang and Bhamo, but Xidong belongs to a different Tengchong–Bhamo mountain route and is not on the same continuous Lianghe–Yingjiang–Bhamo segment. Second, it removes Mandalay. Mandalay belongs to the Baoshan–Ruili–Mandalay southern branch; placing it after Bhamo and then returning north to Myitkyina would create a false south-then-north itinerary. Third, it removes the 'Assam tea region' as a terminal station. A tea region is an area, not a city; the article uses Tinsukia, Dibrugarh, Sivasagar and Jorhat as valley nodes and ends at Guwahati, avoiding a return east from Guwahati to the tea belt.

The distance is about 2,850 km on the reordered table. It is not a modern survey and does not match road or straight-line distance exactly; it represents the scale of a historical caravan route, not a navigation figure. Elevations are also rounded reading values: about 55 m at Guwahati and about 2,200 m near Yangbi. The old page's '3,374-metre Gaoligong' conflated a mountain summit with the altitude of the route. This article separates the summit from the pass and does not treat 3,374 m as the height a caravan crossed every day.

Stations

The stations below use the same node_id as the route fact table. The map and elevation profile are reading aids, not a substitute for field data.

01 · Chengdu · Jinguan City

Chengdu was the centre of the Chengdu plain and the common starting point of several Southern Silk Road lines. The Han court established a brocade office in the city, which gave it the name 'Jinguan City'. Shu brocade, Shu cloth, bamboo and wooden goods, ironware and later tea were gathered and distributed here. Calling Chengdu the 'single kilometre-zero of the Southern Silk Road' is a thematic convenience: routes left the city by different roads for different destinations, and caravans chose their exit according to where they were going.

02 · Shuangliu · Wanli Bridge

Wanli Bridge was an old bridge over the Jin River outside Chengdu's south gate. In the Three Kingdoms period Zhuge Liang saw off Fei Yi there; Fei Yi said that a journey of ten thousand li began at this bridge, and the name stuck. In the Tang–Song and later periods the bridgehead became a landmark for seeing off travellers and starting shipments. It is included as a symbol of leaving Chengdu southward; there is no evidence that every caravan crossed this bridge, or that the teahouses at the bridgehead formed a fixed tea market.

03 · Xinjin · Wujin Ferry

Xinjin lies among the ferry crossings of the Min River, the first waterway node south of Chengdu. The river's channels and shoals shifted over time, so the ancient ferry sites cannot be reduced to one modern pier. 'Wujin' means the five fords, a summary name for several crossings, not five permanent docks that all existed at once. After crossing, travellers could head toward Ya'an, Meishan or Leshan by different roads.

04 · Ya'an · Rain City

Ya'an sits in the transition zone between the Chengdu plain and the Tibetan plateau, where the Lingguan Road overlaps with the Sichuan–Tibet Tea Horse Road. Mengding Mountain in Ya'an is an important Sichuan tea-producing site, and Mengding tea became famous in the Tang. Ya'an Tibetan tea, or Nanbian route tea, was a product of the tea–horse trade after the Song. Calling Ya'an 'the tea warehouse of the Shu-Shendu road' and treating border tea as the main cargo of the Southern Silk Road confuses two directions: Ya'an border tea went north into Kham and Tibet, while the Lingguan Road went southwest into Yunnan.

05 · Yingjing · Yandao

Yingjing, anciently Yandao, was a mountain county on the Lingguan Road. Its sand pottery is a local craft, and sand pots were later used to boil tea; there is no trade record to make pottery the 'core commodity' of the route. The stone-paved roads, post-station sites and Yandao old city are useful evidence for studying the Lingguan Road, but a few horse prints cannot measure the trade volume of an entire route.

06 · Hanyuan · Qingxi Pass

The Qingxi Pass area of Hanyuan was a traditional crossing of the Daxiangling range. The old page called it 'the first pass of the Southern Silk Road'; such titles belong to local narrative. The exact pass site, its walls and the road alignment must be checked against cultural-relic surveys and local gazetteers. Daxiangling is high, with winter snow and monsoon landslides; caravans changed horses, reduced loads or took alternative passes.

07 · Yuexi · Lingguan Road

The Lingguan Road section in Yuexi preserves stone paving, steps and horse prints. The prints show that the route was used repeatedly; they do not show how many loads of tea passed each year. The name Lingguan comes from a pass in Yuexi, but different dynasties may have used the name for different places. Treating every 'Lingguan' as the same fortress is a common mistake in place-name history.

08 · Xichang · Qiongdu

Xichang, anciently Qiongdu, lies in the Anning River valley and was one of the largest transfer points on the Lingguan Road. From Xichang one could go south through Huili and Panzhihua into Yunnan, or west through Yanyuan, Ninglang and Lijiang into northwest Yunnan. The old page claimed that Xichang had 'the largest horse market in southwest China' without a statistical source. What can be confirmed is its long role as an administrative, military and commercial centre serving several routes.

09 · Dechang · Phoenix Town

Dechang lies in the Anning River valley between Xichang and Huili and is locally called Phoenix Town. The old page said it had a 'Tea Shop Street' and a famous caravan tea market where Yi headmen traded good horses for Sichuan tea; these details have no gazetteer or archival citation and are not retained. Dechang's importance is as a valley supply point for fodder, grain, horse rest and escape from the mountain tracks, not as a named tea market.

10 · Huili · Old Town

Huili old town was an important node between Sichuan and Yunnan and a commercial town on the Southern Silk Road. The old page called it the 'junction of the Lingguan and Wuchi roads', which is geographically inaccurate: the Wuchi Road ran mainly through Yibin, Zhaotong, Qujing and Kunming to Dali, while the Lingguan Road ran through Xichang, Huili and Panzhihua to Dali; the two met near Dali, not at Huili. Huili's importance comes from its position on the Sichuan–Yunnan corridor, not from being the 'only junction of two official roads'.

11 · Panzhihua · Lazha Ferry

Lazha Ferry lies near Panzhihua on the Jinsha River. Caravans entering Yunnan from Sichuan crossed here and then went through Yongren and Dayao toward Dali. The old page said the cliff at the ferry bore the words 'Shu-Shendu Road' and inscriptions from various dynasties, but gave no rubbing or heritage record. This article treats Lazha as a Jinsha crossing and leaves the exact ancient ferry site and cliff inscriptions to local cultural-relic authorities.

12 · Yongren · Fangshan

Yongren lies on the Yunnan–Sichuan border, and the Fangshan area is mountain country crossed by old tracks. Stone paving, post-station remains and caravan memories survive locally; they show that Fangshan was a transit node, not that every caravan stayed there. The old page's line about 'horse bells on the Fangshan road, tea fragrance across the Yunnan–Sichuan border' is literary couplet-writing, not a historical quotation.

13 · Dayao · Shiyang Old Town

Shiyang old town in Dayao is known for its salt wells. Salt was a necessity of long-distance trade; Shiyang salt moved south along the old roads and was exchanged for Sichuan tea, cloth and ironware in local markets. 'Tea and salt belong to one family' is a later summary of household needs, not a fixed entry in ancient ledgers. Shiyang can be called a salt town of the Tea Horse Road, but not a place where every tea caravan also carried salt.

14 · Dali · Yeyu

Dali, anciently Yeyu, was the capital of the Nanzhao and Dali kingdoms and a junction of several western Yunnan roads. The basin between the Cangshan range and Erhai Lake connects eastward to Kunming, westward to Baoshan and Tengchong, and southward to Pu'er and Xishuangbanna. The Yunnan–Tibet Tea Horse Road, the western Southern Silk Road and the Yunnan–Burma trade routes overlap around Dali, but overlap does not make them one road. Dali is better understood as a branching hub than as the single confluence of two ancient roads.

15 · Yangbi · Shimen Pass

Yangbi lies on the western slope of the Cangshan range, with the Yangbi River running through it. Travelling west from Dali, caravans followed the valley into the mountains; the Shimen Pass gorge was one traditional corridor. The old page said the cliffs at Shimen Pass bore Tang–Song caravan inscriptions and dated records, but gave no rubbing, transcription or protected-site record; this article does not treat the claim as fact. Walnuts, medicinal herbs, timber and tea were all common mountain trade goods in Yangbi.

16 · Yongping · Bonan Mountain

The Bonan Road in Yongping takes its name from Bonan Mountain and is a section of the ancient Southern Silk Road. The track winds along ridges and valleys, and some sections preserve stone paving, steps and post-station remains. The old page called it the 'Hande post road built by Emperor Wu' and quoted the poem 'Han virtue is wide, it opened the unsubmissive; it crossed Bonan and crossed Lanjin'. The poem is often used in accounts of Bonan and Lanjin, but its date, author and exact referent are still debated; it cannot serve as an engineering record of Emperor Wu's road. The Bonan Road's long use is supported by route geography, heritage sites and local gazetteers.

17 · Lanjin Ferry · Jihong Bridge

Jihong Bridge crossed the Lancang between Yongping and Baoshan, and Lanjin Ferry was the old crossing in the same area. This was a key node for the Southern Silk Road: travellers came down from Yongping, crossed the river and climbed into the Baoshan basin. The bridge site was rebuilt many times, and the present structure is not a Han original. Titles such as 'China's oldest iron-chain bridge' have different meanings in different sources. Longyang district records say that Jihong was completely destroyed by the Xiaowan hydropower project and that the Putuo cliff west of the bridge holds 40 carved inscriptions, 30 of them legible. The bridge, the cliff inscriptions and the ferry should be understood as one heritage group, not as a single set of chains.

18 · Baoshan · Yongchang

Baoshan, anciently Yongchang, was the political, military and commercial centre of western Yunnan. The Eastern Han established Yongchang commandery, and later dynasties maintained Yongchang prefecture. The Hanzhuang city site and Guangzun Temple near the Baoshan basin are national key cultural relics, showing that the area was long an administrative and religious centre on the frontier. From Dali to Baoshan, caravans crossed the Lancang and the Nu; from Baoshan to Tengchong, they still had to cross the Gaoligong Mountains. Baoshan was therefore a place to replenish grain, change mules and repair packs, not a city that prospered on tea alone.

19 · Pupiao · Ancient Post Station

Pupiao lies between Baoshan and the Nu River, and the old page describes it as a post-station settlement. Local memories of horse inns, hot springs and road services suggest that it provided lodging, washing and fodder; whether 'rows of horse inns' can be documented requires local gazetteers and heritage surveys. Calling Pupiao an 'international hotel' is tourism writing; calling it a supply point on the Baoshan–Tengchong road is safer.

20 · Nu River · Huiren Bridge

The Huiren Bridge site in Daojie village, Lujiang township, Longyang district, about 2 km west of the village on the Nu River, is a national key cultural relic within the Tea Horse Road heritage. The bridge was first built in the Qing, rebuilt several times, and long served the southern Baoshan–Tengchong route across the Nu. The gorge is deep and the current fast; in the past, crossings depended on iron-chain bridges, vine bridges or ferries. Caravans unloaded part of their cargo and led horses across in groups to reduce sway. Calling Huiren 'the last river barrier' is literary; the Gaoligong Mountains were the greater obstacle between Baoshan and Tengchong.

21 · Tengchong · Tengyue

Tengchong, anciently Tengyue, was one of the most important border emporia in western Yunnan and is often called the 'first city of the extreme frontier'. From Tengchong one could enter Myanmar to the west or south; to the east the road crossed the Gaoligong Mountains to Baoshan; to the north it went through Pianma toward Putao in northern Myanmar; to the south it went through Lianghe and Yingjiang to Bhamo. The Tengchong section of the Tea Horse Road is part of the seventh batch of national key cultural relics, including the north Zhaigongfang, south Zhaigongfang, Dafengkou and Yubipo stone-paved roads. Heshun is a border emigrant hometown on the Southern Silk Road; its 'zou yifang' networks carried jade, cotton, tea, salt and daily goods in and out. The old page's claim that Tengyue was the largest tea market in southwest China has no statistical source and is not retained.

22 · Lianghe · Nandian

Lianghe lies south of Tengchong. The Nandian chieftain office is one of the better-preserved Qing-era tusi offices in western Yunnan. On the road from Tengchong to Yingjiang, Lianghe was the first valley stop where caravans could switch to lighter equipment, replenish fodder and enter the hotter lower valley. The chieftain office, horse inns, tax posts and border markets together formed this trade order. The old page said that tea merchants paid duty here at one cake per load, turning a complex tusi tax system into a jingle; with no archival support, the claim is not retained.

23 · Yingjiang · Tongbi Pass

Yingjiang lies on the western Yunnan border, and the Daying River flows toward the Irrawaddy. Tongbi Pass was one of the Ming–Qing frontier forts in western Yunnan. Old travellers said that beyond Tongbi Pass lay the 'Yi lands', a summary of the change in language, dress and administration after leaving China, not the modern state border. Today's China–Myanmar boundary is the product of modern treaties and state-building; a Ming–Qing fort cannot be treated as a modern customs post. From Yingjiang one could enter the Bhamo plain in northern Myanmar. This route connects with the traditional Tengchong–Lianghe–Yingjiang caravan road and partly overlaps with the modern Yunnan–Burma highway and border roads.

24 · Bhamo, Myanmar

Bhamo lies on the upper Irrawaddy and is a river port and trading town in northern Myanmar. Caravans from Tengchong and Yingjiang often made Bhamo their first stop; from there they could go south down the Irrawaddy to Mandalay and Yangon, or north to Myitkyina. The old page said that 'half the tea firms in Bhamo were Yunnanese' and that 'tens of thousands of dan of Chinese tea were traded here each year', but gave no reliable statistics. This article describes Bhamo as a river port and border market. Mandalay is a market further south and does not belong to this main chain; the Baoshan–Ruili–Mandalay branch does not pass through Bhamo.

25 · Myitkyina

Myitkyina is the capital of Kachin State and a transport hub on the upper Irrawaddy. The river and road between Myitkyina and Bhamo served different functions in different periods, and the twentieth-century Stilwell Road made Myitkyina a base for the Allied reconquest of Burma. Burmese laphet, or pickled tea, is made in Myitkyina and elsewhere in Shan State and upper Burma, but it is a local tea food, not a Pu'er variant. The old page claimed that Yunnanese migrants exchanged Pu'er techniques with laphet makers; there is no historical evidence, and this article keeps the two tea-food traditions separate.

26 · Pangsau Pass

The Pangsau Pass crosses the Patkai range on the Indo–Myanmar border and is one of the passes from Myanmar into northeast India. Its elevation is about 1,100 m. In tropical rainforest that is not high, but the track was rough, muddy in the monsoon and malarial; both caravans and armies found it difficult. During the Second World War the Stilwell Road ran from Ledo through the Pangsau Pass into Burma. The road made the pass a military transport node, but it was not the only ancient gateway into India. Calling it 'the natural gate for ancient caravans' ignores other passes and sea routes.

27 · Ledo, India

Ledo, in the extreme northeast of Assam, was the starting point of the Stilwell Road. In 1942 China, the United States and Britain decided to build a road from Ledo through Myitkyina to Kunming; Ledo became a wartime logistics base. In the twentieth century it became a rear base because of the road and the war. Its surroundings had tea gardens, coal fields and railways, but its reputation comes from the road, not from tea planting. There is no reliable record that ancient caravans made Ledo a fixed stop; this article treats it as a twentieth-century road node while acknowledging that it lies at the western end of an older cross-border corridor.

28 · Tinsukia

Tinsukia is a tea and industrial town in eastern Assam. The Indian Tea Board describes Assam as one of the world's largest tea-growing regions; the upper Assam belt around Dibrugarh, Tinsukia, Sivasagar and Jorhat is a core tea area. Coming down from the Pangsau Pass into the Brahmaputra valley, Tinsukia is the first substantial town node, with tea gardens, factories, railways and roads to the Myanmar border. The old station table omitted Tinsukia and jumped from Ledo to Dibrugarh, missing the eastern upper-Assam tea belt; this article adds the station.

29 · Dibrugarh

Dibrugarh lies on the Brahmaputra and is an important tea city and river port of upper Assam. The Indian Tea Board describes Assam as one of the world's largest tea-growing regions; after the commercial tea gardens of the nineteenth century, this area became a core of tea growing, processing and export. Dibrugarh's 'tea city' label comes from the modern industry, not from ancient caravan trade. The old page turned the colonial 'discovery of wild tea' story into the industry's origin; this article only treats the establishment of nineteenth-century commercial gardens as a verifiable time marker.

30 · Sivasagar

Sivasagar was the capital of the Ahom kingdom and contains the Rang Ghar, Talatal Ghar and Sivadol. The Charaideo royal burial mounds lie about 28 km east of the town. The Indian Press Information Bureau's research brief says that Tai-Ahom migrants came from China and established capitals in the Brahmaputra valley between the twelfth and eighteenth centuries; Choraideo became the first capital and royal burial ground under Chaulung Siu-ka-pha, and the tradition lasted about 600 years. This historical layer is centuries removed from the nineteenth-century tea gardens. The claim that the Ahom brought tea-drinking customs to Assam has no reliable historical basis and is not retained.

31 · Jorhat

Jorhat is an important centre of tea research, management and trade in upper Assam. It has tea research institutions, garden-management offices and tea auction facilities; Tocklai is the oldest and largest research station of its kind, long engaged in tea breeding, cultivation and processing research. The old page said that Tocklai held Yunnan tea-processing archives, but gave no source; this article describes Jorhat as a service centre of the modern tea industry. Sivasagar and Jorhat are close together; the article places Sivasagar before Jorhat in the east-to-west reading order to avoid a map that goes from Jorhat back to Sivasagar.

32 · Guwahati

Guwahati is the largest city in Assam, on the Brahmaputra, and the transport, commercial and educational centre of northeast India. It was anciently Pragjyotishpura and is associated with the Kamarupa kingdom; Xuanzang's Datang Xiyu Ji records Kamarupa, whose territory is associated with western Assam. In modern times Guwahati became a collection and distribution centre for tea, oil, timber and border trade, and the gateway from Assam to the rest of India. The old page claimed that 'more than 60 percent of all Indian black tea is exported through Guwahati', but gave no reliable statistic, and the claim is not retained. Guwahati is the endpoint because it is a valley port and transport hub, not because it is the site of a particular tea garden.

Teas traded

  • Mengding tea. A famous Sichuan tea from Mengding Mountain in Ya'an, sent as tribute in the Tang and important in Chinese tea-cultural history. Its connection with the Southern Silk Road is a connection of production culture, not a ledger entry showing it as the route's main export. There is no evidence for the old claim that Mengding Ganlu was 'the most precious cargo of the Shu-Shendu road'.
  • Ya'an Tibetan tea (Nanbian route tea). A border tea that developed after the Song tea–horse law. In the Qianlong period the counties of Ya'an, Tianquan and Yingjing were assigned coarse tea for the Kham–Tibet market. It travelled mainly north along the Sichuan–Tibet Tea Horse Road to Kangding and the Tibetan lands, not west along the Shu-Shendu road into Myanmar. Its 32-step processing was shaped by long transport and storage.
  • Pu'er tea. A traditional Yunnan compressed tea from Pu'er, Xishuangbanna, Lincang and nearby areas. From the Qing to the Republican period it went north to Tibet and south to Myanmar, Siam and Vietnam; in the 1930s and 1940s much of it still travelled through Myanmar and India to Tibet. Sheng Pu'er ages naturally; shou Pu'er is artificially fermented, and the wo dui process developed in the later twentieth century. Shou Pu'er cannot be treated as an ancient caravan product.
  • Xiaguan tuocha. A bowl-and-mortar-shaped compressed tea developed in 1902 from older cake tea, easy to stack and transport by pack animal. It was sold in western Yunnan, Tibet, Sichuan and Myanmar. Calling it 'invented for the Southern Silk Road' reverses cause and effect; its shape was shaped by several long-distance transport needs.
  • Dali Cangshan Xue Lu. A modern Dali green tea; its name and processing belong to contemporary tea production. It can represent Dali tea culture but cannot be projected back as a Han-era caravan good.
  • Assam black tea. A black tea produced by the commercial gardens that developed in the nineteenth century; Assam is now one of the world's largest tea-growing regions. Its industrial history involves colonial capital, introduced planting material, railways and ports. It is not a direct descendant of the Yunnan tea road.

Tea was not the only cargo. Salt, cotton yarn, raw cotton, jade, ironware, medicinal herbs, sea products and daily goods also moved through these passes and ferries. Tea stood out because it was compact, valuable and easy to compress for pack transport, but reducing the whole corridor to 'a road for tea' narrows the history in the same way.

Visiting notes

This page is historical geography, not a trekking guide, border-pass guide or modern navigation tool. The Southern Silk Road crosses China, Myanmar and India and passes through border areas, ethnic armed-group territories, monsoon landslide zones and tropical disease zones. Do not leave the road and enter unfamiliar mountains on the basis of the schematic map, and do not treat old caravan tracks as walkable cross-border routes.

In China, Chengdu, Ya'an, Xichang, Huili, Dali, Baoshan, Tengchong, Lianghe and Yingjiang are accessible by road. The Bonan Road, Jihong Bridge site, Huiren Bridge and Tengchong Tea Horse Road sections are partly in the open country and partly within scenic areas or protected zones. Jihong's bridge body was completely destroyed by the Xiaowan hydropower project; what visitors see today is the site, the cliff inscriptions and a later reconstructed bridge, not the ancient bridge itself. Observe site notices at protected cultural relics; do not climb, take rubbings or remove stones and bridge chains. Ask permission before entering tea gardens, chieftain offices, religious sites and private courtyards.

The Myanmar and India sections involve border permits, visas, security conditions and health risks. Access to Bhamo, Myitkyina, the Pangsau Pass, Ledo, Tinsukia, Dibrugarh, Sivasagar, Jorhat and Guwahati changes with local conditions. Before travelling, check Chinese consular-protection information, Myanmar and Indian official notices, and the entry and border-permit rules of the countries concerned. Do not use the old page's 'caravan route' descriptions to plan a modern trip.

Seasonally, the dry period in western Yunnan is roughly November to April, when monsoon landslides are less likely. Assam and northern Myanmar have a longer tropical monsoon, with heat, mosquitoes and malaria risk. Historical caravans chose the dry season; that does not make the monsoon safe for modern travellers. Modern roads, railways and aviation have changed accessibility, but border and mountain risks remain.
