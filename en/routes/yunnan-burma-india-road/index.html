The Yunnan–Burma–India Tea Road

China

The Yunnan–Burma–India Tea Road is a thematic corridor from Dali through Baoshan and Tengchong into Myanmar, over the Patkai Pass, and down the Brahmaputra valley to Guwahati. This article separates the movement of Yunnan tea, Burmese pickled tea, the nineteenth-century Assam plantation industry and the Tai-Ahom migration, and refuses a single-line “Yunnan tea seed to Assam” origin story.

Overview

The Yunnan–Burma–India Tea Road is a cross-border corridor from western Yunnan through northern Myanmar into north-eastern India. Its Chinese section runs roughly from Dali and Xiaguan through Yangbi, Yongping, Baoshan, Tengchong, Lianghe and Yingjiang. The Myanmar section passes through Bhamo and Myitkyina, crosses the Patkai range, and enters India at Ledo. From there the corridor follows Tinsukia, Dibrugarh, Sivasagar and Jorhat down the Brahmaputra valley to Guwahati.

This page uses twenty stations as a reading sample. The station table totals about 2,000 km and gives elevations from about 55 to 2,200 m. Both figures are rounded from the old station table. They are historical scales, not survey results and not modern driving distances.

The name needs a caveat. “Yunnan–Burma–India Tea Road” is not a fixed proper name in ancient documents. It is a modern thematic label for a corridor that carried many goods and many kinds of people. There was no single, imperially maintained road running from Dali to Guwahati. Yunnan tea could leave through Tengchong, Ruili or Wanding; it could go down the Irrawaddy, across the Patkai passes, or by sea. Different dynasties, merchant groups and seasons produced different routes. Drawing one line is a reading device, not a reconstruction of the only possible itinerary.

The corridor is not only about tea. It overlaps the Yongchang road of the old south-western Silk Road, the southern line of the Tea Horse Road, modern Yunnan–Myanmar trade, the Second World War Stilwell Road, and today's border highways. Tea is one of the most recognisable cargoes, but it was never the only one. Calling the corridor a “tea road” does not mean that every segment existed solely to move tea.

History and context

Trade before the name

Connections between Yunnan, Myanmar and north-eastern India are much older than the label. Dali was the capital of the Nanzhao and Dali kingdoms and a junction of several routes. Baoshan, historically Yongchang, was the gateway from western Yunnan into Myanmar. Tengchong was a busy frontier market by the Qing period. These towns were linked by post roads, mountain paths, ferries and inns. Caravans carried tea, salt, cotton yarn, jade, ironware and household goods; return loads included cotton, marine products, medicinal materials and metal tools.

Jiang Wenzhong, writing for the Yunnan Academy of Social Sciences, describes the Tea Horse Road as beginning in the Tang–Song period, with its southern line running from Pu'er and Xishuangbanna into Myanmar and India. He adds that in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the southern Yunnan tea mountains produced more than 100,000 dan a year, much of it sold in South and Southeast Asia. This “southern line” is a trade network, not a single official road with a chain of imperially appointed post stations. It linked the tea districts around Pu'er, Simao, Jinghong and Menghai with markets in Myanmar and India, and it also connected the western Yunnan towns of Tengchong, Baoshan and Dali.

The Pu'er municipal government's account of the Ning'er section pushes the story back to the Tang–Song “tea-horse market” and treats the old Pu'er prefecture seat at Ning'er as the source. Five stone-paved sections survive in Ning'er county. Ning'er was one of the processing and distribution centres of Pu'er tea, but the old Pu'er prefecture and the present-day Pu'er municipality are not the same administrative area. Making Ning'er the sole origin of every southern route would again compress a network into a single line.

From Pu'er to Dali and Xiaguan: packing tea for mules

Traditional Pu'er tea was compressed. Workers steamed loose tea and pressed it into cakes, bricks or bowls. Compression made the tea easier to pack, carry and measure by the load. Dali and Xiaguan were among the most important processing and transshipment markets in western Yunnan. Loose tea from Pu'er, Jinggu, Menghai and other districts arrived there; Xiaguan merchants then pressed it into different shapes for different markets.

The dating of Xiaguan tuocha is comparatively clear. The Yunnan Provincial Department of Industry and Information Technology states that in 1902 pressed tea was shipped to Xiaguan, where local merchants developed the bowl-and-mortar tuocha from the older cake form. The craft was listed as a national intangible cultural heritage in 2011 and as a UNESCO intangible cultural heritage in 2022. The shape — a hollow centre with a thicker rim — made the cakes stack and load well. It was a response to transport and commerce, not the invention of a legendary figure.

“Pu'er” and “Dianhong” must be kept apart. Modern Pu'er includes sheng (raw) and shou (ripe) types; the wo dui fermentation process for shou Pu'er matured only in the later twentieth century. Dianhong is a black tea developed around Fengqing in the twentieth century. Calling Dianhong an ancient cargo of a thousand-year tea road is a chronological error. The section on trade teas below treats these categories separately.

The Bonan road, Jihong Bridge and Baoshan: ferries and post stations on the Yongchang road

West of Dali, the first section crosses the Cangshan range and the Yangbi River into Yongping. The Bonan Ancient Road, named for the Bonan mountain it crosses, is a section of the old south-western Silk Road. After descending from Yongping, caravans crossed the Lancang River at the Jihong Bridge and Lanjin ferry, then entered the Baoshan basin. Jihong Bridge is an old iron-chain bridge site on the Lancang. The bridge was rebuilt many times; the structure visible today is not a Han-dynasty original. Claims that it is “China's oldest iron-chain bridge” rest on different counting methods in different sources, and this page does not treat the title as a fixed date.

Baoshan, historically Yongchang, was the political, military and commercial centre of western Yunnan. To go west from Baoshan, travellers had to cross the Gaoligong Mountains to reach Tengchong. The Gaoligong is not a single peak but a north–south range; several old roads crossed it by different passes and post stations. The Tengchong municipal government lists the Tengchong section of the Tea Horse Road as part of the seventh batch of national key cultural relics, including the Beizhaigongfang, Nanzhaigongfang, Dafenkou and Yubipo stone-paved roads. These are protected heritage sections, not the whole of a single caravan main line.

The Nu River is another gate between Baoshan and Tengchong. The Huiren Bridge site lies on the Nu River about 2 km west of Daojie village in Lujiang township, Longyang district, Baoshan. It is a national key cultural relic and part of the Tea Horse Road heritage. The bridge was first built in the Qing period and rebuilt several times; it long served the southern Baoshan–Tengchong post road at the Nu River crossing. Calling it “the last river gorge” is literary language. Between Baoshan and Tengchong there are also the Gaoligong range, the Longchuan River and many smaller ridges.

Tengchong, Lianghe and Yingjiang: layers of a frontier market

Tengchong was one of the most important frontier markets in western Yunnan, locally called the “first town at the extreme frontier”. From Tengchong, one route went south through Lianghe and Yingjiang into Myanmar; another, more familiar to modern travellers, leaves through Ruili and Wanding. Both lead toward Bhamo and Myitkyina in northern Myanmar, but they pass through different valleys, border posts and ethnic areas. They should not be merged into one line.

The Nandian chiefdom office at Lianghe is one of the better-preserved chiefly offices in western Yunnan. The tusi system lasted for a long time in the borderlands; caravans crossing a chiefdom had to deal with tolls, inns, local headmen and an order that was more complex than a “free market”. The old page's line that “one cake of tea was taken from every load” turns a complex fiscal arrangement into a jingle. There is no archival support for it, and this page does not retain it.

Tongbi Pass in Yingjiang was one of the frontier defences in the Ming–Qing border system. The old travellers' phrase “beyond Tongbi Pass lies the foreign land” summarised the change in language, dress and administration after crossing, not a modern international boundary. Today's China–Myanmar border is a product of modern treaties and state-building. A Ming–Qing pass cannot be treated as a modern customs post.

Myanmar: Bhamo, Myitkyina and pickled tea

Bhamo is a river port and trading town on the upper Irrawaddy. Caravans from Tengchong and Yingjiang often made it their first stop in Myanmar. From Bhamo, goods could go south down the Irrawaddy to Mandalay and Yangon, or north to Myitkyina. In the nineteenth century British colonial officials tried to open a trade route from Burma to Yunnan, and Bhamo became a meeting point for surveys, missions, commerce and military planning. The old page's claim that “half the tea shops in Bhamo were run by Yunnanese” has no reliable statistic behind it, and this page does not repeat it.

Myitkyina is the capital of Kachin State and a transport hub on the upper Irrawaddy. The river and road links between Myitkyina and Bhamo served different purposes in different periods. In the twentieth century the China–India road made Myitkyina a Allied staging point for the reconquest of Burma. Calling it “the centre of Pu'er tea in Myanmar” exaggerates the weight of one commodity. It was a junction for many goods, soldiers, refugees and administrators.

Burmese laphet is an important local tea food. Fresh tea leaves are steamed, kneaded and fermented, then mixed with oil, garlic, dried shrimp and peanuts, or made into a tea-leaf salad. It is not the same as Pu'er in either processing or consumption. Because both are called “tea”, one cannot claim that “Yunnan Pu'er techniques and Burmese laphet exchanged methods to create a Sino-Burmese tea culture”. Myanmar's tea culture has its own upland and court traditions and deserves to be told on its own terms, not as an appendix to a Yunnan route.

Assam: the Tai-Ahom migration and the nineteenth-century garden

Assam's history has two layers that must be kept apart. The first is the political history of the Ahom kingdom. A 22 July 2024 research brief from the Press Information Bureau states that the Tai-Ahom migrated from China and established capitals in the Brahmaputra Valley between the twelfth and eighteenth centuries. Choraideo became their first capital and royal necropolis under Chaulung Siu-ka-pha, and the burial tradition lasted about 600 years. This migration is one of the most important human links between Assam and Yunnan, but it concerns a political community and its language and culture, not the transmission of tea plants or tea-drinking customs.

The second layer is the commercial tea industry. Tea Board India's Assam page describes Assam as one of the world's largest tea-growing regions, with Tocklai as the oldest and largest research station of its kind. Assam produces both orthodox and CTC tea, and Assam orthodox tea has a registered geographical indication. This industry was the product of the colonial economy, local wild tea resources, introduced planting material and processing knowledge. It cannot be reduced to “the Yunnan tea road brought tea plants directly to Assam”.

A Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs travel essay on Assam offers a useful sidelight. Local hosts said that their ancestors had migrated from south-west China in the early thirteenth century and founded the Ahom kingdom. The same essay notes that Assam tea was propagated from Fujian seed in the nineteenth century. This separates the migration of people from the introduction of planting material: the Ahom ancestry story points to Dehong in Yunnan, while one nineteenth-century source of tea seed points to Fujian. Both can be true, but neither can substitute for the other. Neither supports a claim that “Assam tea is the direct descendant of Yunnan tea carried along this road two thousand years ago”.

The old page turned the colonial “discovery” story into the origin of the Assam tea industry; this page does not adopt that single-line origin. Assam does have native large-leaf tea resources, but the starting point of the commercial industry should be placed in the establishment of commercial gardens in the nineteenth century, not in a particular “discovery”.

The twentieth-century road layer: the Stilwell Road is not the ancient tea road

During the Second World War, China, the United States, Britain and other Allies built the China–India road, also called the Stilwell Road, across the Yunnan–Burma–India theatre. The Party history site Qiyi Wang states that construction began in 1942; on 27 January 1945 Chinese forces from India and the Chinese Expeditionary Force met at Mongyu near Wanding, opening the road. The route ran from Ledo in India through Myitkyina in Burma to Kunming in Yunnan, a total of 1,736 km, and carried more than 50,000 tonnes of supplies to the Chinese war effort.

The road overlaps the old trade corridor geographically, but the two belong to different eras. Ancient caravans used mountain paths, post roads and ferries; the twentieth-century road used bulldozers, bridges, trucks and airfields. The old caravans carried tea, salt and cotton; the wartime convoys carried petrol, ammunition, medical supplies and troops. To call the Stilwell Road “a continuation of the thousand-year tea road” erases the differences in technology, politics and the nature of war. A more accurate description is that the two layers sit in the same geographic corridor, each belonging to its own historical period.

Route and scope

The twenty stations on this page form an east-to-west reading sample:

Dali (Nanzhao capital) → Xiaguan (tuocha processing and transshipment) → Yangbi (Shimen Pass and the Yangbi River) → Yongping (Bonan Ancient Road) → Jihong Bridge and Lanjin ferry (Lancang River) → Baoshan (Yongchang) → Huiren Bridge on the Nu River → Longling → Tengchong (frontier market) → Lianghe (Nandian) → Yingjiang (Tongbi Pass) → Bhamo (Irrawaddy) → Myitkyina → Pangsau Pass → Ledo → Tinsukia → Dibrugarh → Sivasagar → Jorhat → Guwahati.

This is not the only route. From Yunnan, one could also leave through Ruili, Muse, Lashio and Mandalay, or through Menghai, Kengtung and northern Thailand. From Myanmar to India, one could go through Manipur or Mizoram, or take a sea route. The Tengchong–Bhamo–Myitkyina–Pangsau line is singled out because it recurs in the southern Tea Horse Road, modern Yunnan–Myanmar trade and the Stilwell Road, and because the sources are relatively concentrated. It makes a good reading sample, not a claim that every caravan took it.

Distances follow the old station table's rounded figures: about 2,000 km from Dali to Guwahati. This figure was not produced by a modern survey and does not match modern road distances or straight-line distances. It indicates the scale of a historical trade route, not a navigation distance. Station elevations are also rounded reading values: the lowest is about 55 m at Guwahati, the highest about 2,200 m near Yangbi. They do not claim precision for every segment. The old page once wrote “Gaoligong 3,374 m”, confusing a mountain summit with a route elevation. This page separates the summit from the pass and no longer treats 3,374 m as the height a caravan crossed every day.

The station table also makes one structural correction. The old table placed “Xidong” between Yingjiang and Bhamo. Xidong is a pass on a different border route, not on the Tengchong–Lianghe–Yingjiang–Bhamo line. This page removes Xidong, adds Longling, and changes the terminus from “Assam tea region” to Guwahati. Guwahati is a river port and transport hub in the Brahmaputra valley; it makes a better urban terminus than a generic “tea region”, and it avoids the old table's geographic backtracking from Guwahati to the tea districts.

Stations

The stations below use the same node_id as the route fact table. The map and elevation profile are reading aids, not a substitute for field data.

01 · Dali (Nanzhao Ancient Capital)

Dali was the capital of the Nanzhao and Dali kingdoms and a junction of several routes in western Yunnan. The basin between Cangshan and Erhai Lake connects east to Kunming, west to Baoshan and Tengchong, and south to Pu'er and Xishuangbanna. Calling Dali the “northern origin” of the Yunnan–Burma–India Tea Road is a thematic device. In practice, Pu'er tea, Xiaguan tuocha, Tibetan border tea and Burmese goods all changed hands here. Dali was a branching hub, not the start of a single line.

02 · Xiaguan (Tuocha Processing and Transshipment)

Xiaguan sits near the outlet of Erhai Lake, at the windy gap between the southern Cangshan and the lake. In 1902 pressed tea was shipped to Xiaguan, where local merchants developed the bowl-and-mortar tuocha from the older cake form. The shape made the tea easy to stack, load and measure. The craft was listed as national intangible heritage in 2011 and as UNESCO intangible heritage in 2022. Xiaguan still has tea factories, tea markets and tuocha workshops, but claims that a “century-old wooden press is still running” belong to tourism storytelling, not production history.

03 · Yangbi (Shimen Pass and the Yangbi River)

Yangbi lies on the western slope of Cangshan, where the Yangbi River cuts through the mountains. Travellers heading west from Dali followed the valley into the uplands; the Shimen Pass gorge was one of the traditional passages. Yangbi is also a district where Yi, Bai and other communities live, and walnuts, medicinal herbs and tea are common mountain trade goods. The old page claimed that Tang–Song cliff inscriptions recorded caravan prayers at Shimen Pass, but gave no rubbings, transcription or protected-site record. This page does not treat that claim as history.

04 · Yongping (Bonan Ancient Road)

The Bonan Ancient Road in Yongping is named for the Bonan mountain it crosses and is a section of the old south-western Silk Road. The road winds along ridges and valleys; some sections retain stone paving, steps and post-station remains. The old page called it a “Han De post road built by Emperor Wu of Han” and quoted the ancient ballad “Han De is wide, opens the unfriendly; cross Bonan, cross Lanjin”. The ballad is often used to illustrate the Bonan and Lanjin crossings, but its date, author and exact referent are debated. It is not an engineering record of Emperor Wu's road. What is clear is that the Bonan road long served the overland route from western Yunnan to Yongchang and Tengyue.

05 · Jihong Bridge and Lanjin Ferry (Lancang River)

Jihong Bridge crosses the Lancang River between Yongping and Baoshan; the Lanjin ferry belongs to the same crossing complex. This was a key point on the old south-western Silk Road: travellers descended from Yongping, crossed the river, and climbed into the Baoshan basin. The bridge was rebuilt many times; the structure visible today is not a Han-dynasty original. The title “China's oldest iron-chain bridge” depends on which source and which counting method one uses. The cliff inscriptions near the bridge are important material for the history of the road and ferry, but the claim that they run “without a break for two thousand years from Han to the Republic” needs a full transcription and dating, not a slogan.

06 · Baoshan (Yongchang Ancient City)

Baoshan, historically Yongchang, was the political, military and commercial centre of western Yunnan. From Dali to Baoshan, caravans crossed the Lancang and Nu river systems; from Baoshan to Tengchong, they had to cross the Gaoligong Mountains. Baoshan was therefore a place to buy grain, change mules and repair packs. The Baoshan basin also contains national key cultural relics such as the Hanzhuang city site and the Guangzun Temple, evidence that it was long an administrative and religious centre on the frontier. The old page called “Baoshan black tea” an important part of Dianhong, conflating the twentieth-century black-tea industry with the ancient route. This page treats Baoshan tea under modern and contemporary products.

07 · Huiren Bridge on the Nu River

The Huiren Bridge site lies on the Nu River about 2 km west of Daojie village, Lujiang township, Longyang district, Baoshan. It is a national key cultural relic within the Tea Horse Road heritage. The bridge was first built in the Qing period and rebuilt several times; it long carried the southern Baoshan–Tengchong post road across the Nu River. The gorge is deep and the current fast. In the past, crossings depended on iron-chain bridges, rope bridges or ferries; mules sometimes had to be unloaded and led across in small groups. Calling Huiren “the last river gorge” is literary language. The Gaoligong Mountains, not the Nu River, were the greater topographic obstacle between Baoshan and Tengchong.

08 · Longling

Longling lies between the Nu River and the Gaoligong Mountains and was an important transition on the Baoshan–Tengchong road. The old station table did not list Longling; it jumped from Huiren Bridge to Tengchong, skipping the mountain paths and basins in between. Longling also contains the Songshan battlefield, a major site of the 1944 counter-offensive in western Yunnan. Songshan belongs to the twentieth-century war layer, not the ancient post road. Longling is added here so that the geography of “cross the Nu, climb the Gaoligong, reach Tengchong” is not compressed into a straight line.

09 · Tengchong (Frontier Trading Town)

Tengchong was one of the most important frontier markets in western Yunnan, locally called the “first town at the extreme frontier”. From Tengchong one could go west or south into Myanmar; east over the Gaoligong to Baoshan; north through Pianma to Putao in northern Myanmar; or south through Lianghe and Yingjiang to Bhamo. The Tengchong section of the Tea Horse Road is part of the seventh batch of national key cultural relics, including the Beizhaigongfang, Nanzhaigongfang, Dafenkou and Yubipo stone-paved roads. The old page claimed that the Tengyue tea market traded “tens of thousands of dan” a year but gave no year, unit or statistical source. This page treats Tengchong as a frontier market and does not repeat the unverified volume.

10 · Lianghe (Nandian)

Lianghe lies south of Tengchong. The Nandian chiefdom office is one of the better-preserved chiefly offices in western Yunnan. For caravans going from Tengchong to Yingjiang, Lianghe was the first valley station: a place to change into lighter clothing, buy fodder and enter the hotter downstream valley. Chiefly offices, inns, toll posts and border markets together made up this trade order. The old page said that “tea merchants paid a tax here, one cake per load”, turning a complex chiefly fiscal system into a jingle. There is no archival support, and this page does not retain it.

11 · Yingjiang (Tongbi Pass)

Yingjiang lies in the western Yunnan borderland, where the Daying River flows toward the Irrawaddy. Tongbi Pass was one of the frontier defences in the Ming–Qing border system. The old travellers' phrase “beyond Tongbi Pass lies the foreign land” described the change in language, dress and administration after crossing, not today's international boundary. From Yingjiang one can enter the Bhamo plain in northern Myanmar. This route connects the traditional Tengchong–Lianghe–Yingjiang caravan line and partly overlaps the modern Yunnan–Myanmar highway.

12 · Bhamo (Irrawaddy River)

Bhamo is a river port and trading town on the upper Irrawaddy in northern Myanmar. Caravans from Tengchong and Yingjiang often made it their first stop in Myanmar. From Bhamo, goods could go south down the Irrawaddy to Mandalay and Yangon, or north to Myitkyina. In the nineteenth century British colonial officials tried to open a trade route from Burma to Yunnan, and Bhamo became a meeting point for surveys, missions, commerce and military planning. The old page claimed that “half the tea shops in Bhamo were run by Yunnanese”, but gave no reliable statistic. This page describes Bhamo as a river port and border market, not as a Yunnanese tea colony.

13 · Myitkyina

Myitkyina is the capital of Kachin State and a transport hub on the upper Irrawaddy. The river and road links between Myitkyina and Bhamo served different purposes at different times; in the twentieth century the China–India road made Myitkyina an Allied staging point for the reconquest of Burma. Burmese laphet is made in Myitkyina, Shan State and other parts of upper Burma, but it is a local tea food, not a variant of Pu'er. The old page claimed that Yunnanese migrants “exchanged Pu'er techniques with laphet”, but there is no historical support. This page keeps the two tea-food traditions separate.

14 · Pangsau Pass

Pangsau Pass lies in the Patkai range on the India–Myanmar border and is one of the passes into north-eastern India. At about 1,100 m it is not high for a tropical range, but the road is rough, the monsoon turns it to mud, and malaria was a historic hazard for caravans and armies. During the Second World War the China–India road crossed the Patkai near here, making the pass a military transport point. It was not the only ancient gateway into India. Calling Pangsau “the natural gateway for ancient tea caravans” ignores other passes and the sea route.

15 · Ledo

Ledo, in the north-east of Assam, was the starting point of the China–India road. In 1942 China, the United States, Britain and other Allies decided to build a road from Ledo through Myitkyina to Kunming; Ledo changed from a small frontier town into a wartime logistics base. Ledo's surrounding area had tea gardens, coal and a railway, but its fame comes mainly from the road and the war, not from tea planting. There is also no reliable record that ancient caravans treated Ledo as a fixed station. This page presents Ledo as a twentieth-century road-layer station while acknowledging that it lies at the western end of the older cross-border corridor.

16 · Tinsukia

Tinsukia is a tea and industrial town in Upper Assam. Tea Board India describes Assam as one of the world's largest tea-growing regions, with Dibrugarh, Tinsukia, Sivasagar and Jorhat as core Upper Assam districts. Coming down from the Patkai into the Brahmaputra valley, Tinsukia is the first substantial urban node; the district has tea gardens, tea factories, railways and roads toward the Myanmar border. The old station table did not list Tinsukia; it jumped from Ledo to Dibrugarh and skipped the eastern Assam tea belt. This page adds the station.

17 · Dibrugarh

Dibrugarh lies on the Brahmaputra and is an important tea city and river port in Upper Assam. Tea Board India describes Assam as one of the world's largest tea-growing regions; after commercial gardens were established in the nineteenth century, the area became a centre of tea growing, processing and export. Dibrugarh's “tea city” title belongs to the modern industry, not to the ancient caravan trade. The old page said that Robert Bruce discovered wild tea here in 1823 and that the Assam tea industry was born from that discovery. That turns a colonial discovery story into an industrial origin. This page treats the establishment of commercial gardens in the nineteenth century as the industry's verifiable starting point.

18 · Sivasagar

Sivasagar was the capital of the Ahom kingdom. The town contains the Rang Ghar, Talatal Ghar and Sivadol; the Charaideo royal necropolis lies about 28 km east of the town. A 2024 Press Information Bureau research brief states that the Tai-Ahom migrated from China and established capitals in the Brahmaputra Valley between the twelfth and eighteenth centuries; Choraideo became their first capital and royal necropolis under Chaulung Siu-ka-pha, and the burial tradition lasted about 600 years. That historical layer is several centuries earlier than the nineteenth-century tea gardens. The claim that the Ahom “brought tea-drinking customs to Assam” has no reliable historical support, and this page does not adopt it.

19 · Jorhat

Jorhat is an important centre of tea research, administration and trade in Upper Assam. It has tea research institutions, garden-management offices and tea auctioneering; the Tocklai Tea Research Institute is located near Jorhat and has long worked on tea breeding, cultivation and processing. The old page claimed that Tocklai held “Yunnan tea-making archives”, but gave no source. This page describes Jorhat as a service centre of the modern tea industry. Jorhat and Sivasagar are close together; this page places Sivasagar before Jorhat, following the east-to-west direction of travel and avoiding a map that backtracks east from Jorhat.

20 · Guwahati

Guwahati is the largest city in Assam, on the Brahmaputra, and the transport, commercial and educational centre of north-eastern India. It was historically known as Pragjyotishpura and is associated with the Kamarupa kingdom; Xuanzang's Great Tang Records on the Western Regions describes Kamarupa, whose territory is connected with western Assam. In modern times Guwahati became a collection and distribution centre for tea, oil, timber and border trade, and the gateway from Assam to the rest of India. The old page claimed that “more than 60 per cent of India's black tea was exported through Guwahati”, but gave no reliable statistic. This page does not repeat it. Guwahati is the terminus because it is a river port and transport hub, not because it is the site of a particular tea garden.

Trade teas

  • Pu'er (sheng and shou): a traditional Yunnan compressed tea from Pu'er, Xishuangbanna, Lincang and other districts. Sheng ages naturally; shou is artificially fermented. The shou process matured in the later twentieth century and cannot be treated as an ancient cargo of the tea road. Pu'er could move south into Myanmar and Thailand, north into Tibetan markets, or east to Hong Kong; its flows were never single.
  • Xiaguan tuocha: a bowl-shaped compressed tea developed from cake tea in 1902, easy to stack and load. It sold in western Yunnan, Tibetan areas, Sichuan and Myanmar. Calling it “invented specifically for the Yunnan–Burma–India Tea Road” reverses cause and effect: its shape was shaped by several long-distance transport needs.
  • Fengqing Dianhong: a twentieth-century black tea from Fengqing and nearby districts, known for robust leaves, golden buds and a sweet, malty aroma. Dianhong is less than a century old and cannot be mixed with the Tang–Song Tea Horse Road. It belongs to modern Yunnan tea export and black-tea production.
  • Burmese laphet: fresh tea leaves are steamed, kneaded and fermented, then eaten mixed with oil, garlic, shrimp and peanuts, or made into a tea-leaf salad. It is a Burmese tea-food tradition, different from Pu'er in processing and consumption. The shared word “tea” does not make them the same craft.
  • Assam black tea: a black tea industry that grew from commercial gardens in the nineteenth century. Tea Board India describes Assam as one of the world's largest tea-growing regions, producing both orthodox and CTC tea; Assam orthodox tea has a registered geographical indication. Its history involves the colonial economy, introduced planting material, railways and ports. It cannot be written as a direct descendant of the Yunnan tea road.

The corridor carried more than tea. Salt, cotton yarn, cotton, jade, ironware, medicinal herbs, marine products and daily goods also passed through these passes and ferries. Tea stands out because it was compact, valuable and easy to compress for transport. But reducing the whole corridor to tea would narrow its history.

Visiting and access

This page is a historical-geographical reading, not a trekking guide, a border-permit manual or a modern navigation aid. The corridor crosses China, Myanmar and India and includes border areas, ethnic-army-controlled areas, monsoon landslide zones and tropical disease risks. Do not leave the road and enter unfamiliar mountains on the basis of this diagram, and do not treat the old caravan route as a walkable cross-border trail.

In China, Dali, Xiaguan, Yongping, Baoshan, Tengchong, Lianghe and Yingjiang can be reached by road. Some heritage points, such as the Bonan road, Jihong Bridge, Huiren Bridge and the Tengchong Tea Horse Road sections, are in the open country, while others are in scenic areas or protected zones. Follow site notices, do not climb or rub inscriptions, and do not move stones or bridge chains. Tea gardens, chiefdom offices, religious sites and private courtyards require permission.

The Myanmar and Indian sections involve border controls, visas, security conditions and health risks. Access to Pangsau Pass, Bhamo, Myitkyina, Ledo, Tinsukia, Dibrugarh, Sivasagar, Jorhat and Guwahati can change with the security situation. Before travelling, check the Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs' consular information, Myanmar and Indian government notices, and the entry and border-permit rules of the countries concerned. Do not rely on the old page's “caravan route” description to plan a modern trip.

In western Yunnan the dry season is roughly November to April; landslides are common in the rainy season. Assam and northern Myanmar have a longer tropical monsoon, with heat, mosquitoes and malaria risk. Historical caravans chose the dry season, but that does not make the rainy season safe for modern travellers. Roads, railways and airlines have changed accessibility, but risks in border and mountain areas remain.

Raw Pu-erh

Raw Pu-erh (sheng pu'er) is one of the two product types of geographical-indication Pu-erh: Yunnan large-leaf sun-dried tea, steamed, pressed and dried, without pile fermentation. It starts bright, floral and bitter, then changes with storage — but ageing is a storage outcome, not a standard guarantee.

Fengqing Dianhong

Fengqing Dianhong is a congou black tea from Fengqing, Yunnan, made from large-leaf cultivars. It has plump, downy leaves, a high aroma and a strong, full taste. Check origin, grade and standard on the label; Dianhong is not Pu-erh.
