China

Overview

The Zhangye brick tea road is a late-imperial trade corridor that connected the dark-tea and green-brick-tea districts of Hunan and Hubei with the Hexi Corridor of Gansu. The shared table records ten nodes over about 1,800 kilometres: Anhua in Hunan, Yangloudong in Hubei, Hankou, Xiangyang, Xi’an, Lanzhou, Wuwei, Zhangye, Jiuquan and Jiayuguan.

The route is a northern counterpart to the better-known tea-horse roads of southwest China. Its cargo was compressed brick tea, not loose leaf. Brick tea is steamed, pressed and dried into dense blocks, which makes it easier to transport over long distances and well suited to boiling with milk, butter or salt. The diagram shows the main spine; the old record also describes branches north toward Mongolia and west toward Xinjiang and Dunhuang.

History and background

Zhangye, historically Ganzhou, sits in the middle of the Hexi Corridor, the narrow oasis chain between the Qilian Mountains and the Gobi. The city was a Silk Road junction and a garrison town. In the Ming and Qing periods it became a market for tea, horses, grain, cloth and salt. The old record calls it the “largest tea market” of the corridor; that is a local claim rather than a measured ranking. What is clear is that Zhangye was a major redistribution point between the Chinese interior and the northwest.

The southern end of the route is the tea country of Hunan and Hubei. Anhua produces dark tea, including the compressed “flower” brick tea known for its golden mould spots. Yangloudong, in Hubei, is associated with green brick tea (qingzhuan) and with the “洞茶” trade. The old record says Yangloudong once had more than a hundred tea firms. That number is a local historical estimate; it should not be read as a verified count.

The Han River carried tea north from Hankou to Xiangyang. From there, pack animals and carts moved the bricks through the passes toward Xi’an, Lanzhou and the Hexi Corridor. Lanzhou was a Yellow River crossing and a supply point. Before the modern iron bridge, goods were ferried on inflated sheepskin or oxhide rafts, several skins lashed together under a wooden platform. The old record describes “tea drifting on the Yellow River” when a raft capsized. The raft technology is real; the drifting-tea image is a vivid local story rather than a documented accident record.

The late Qing official Zuo Zongtang is associated with a reform of the northwest tea-ticket system. The old record says he issued a tea regulation, shifted tickets to Hunan merchants and set up a tea office in Lanzhou. The broad outline — a state-licensed tea trade, Hunan merchant networks and a Lanzhou administrative centre — is plausible for the period. The old record’s exact quota of 4,000 jin per ticket is not used here because it cannot be tied to the accessible sources.

The Hexi Corridor section is an oasis chain, not empty desert. Wuwei, Zhangye, Jiuquan and Jiayuguan sit at springs and river crossings; the Qilian snowmelt feeds the fields. The old record’s “golden Zhangye, silver Wuwei” saying reflects the relative prosperity of the two oasis cities. Guild halls built by Shanxi and Shaanxi merchants are part of Zhangye’s old town heritage. The claim that a “tea-horse office” stood in Zhangye is a local historical assertion; the city’s role as a tea market is better documented than the exact office.

Route and scope

The ten recorded nodes are Anhua (0 km, about 100 m), Yangloudong (200 km, about 80 m), Hankou (400 km, about 70 m), Xiangyang (600 km, about 110 m), Xi’an (900 km, about 400 m), Lanzhou (1,100 km, about 1,520 m), Wuwei (1,300 km, about 1,530 m), Zhangye (1,500 km, about 1,480 m), Jiuquan (1,650 km, about 1,470 m) and Jiayuguan (1,800 km, about 1,650 m). The route uses river, road and caravan segments; the distances are historical estimates.

Stations

The stations below use the same node_id as the route fact table. The map and elevation profile are reading aids, not a substitute for field data.

01 · Anhua, Hunan

Anhua is a core district of Hunan dark tea. The Zi River valley and the surrounding hills produce tea that is withered, rolled, piled, fermented and dried; some of it is pressed into bricks. The old record calls Anhua the “birthplace of dark tea” and says the tea-horse road traces are still visible. Anhua is certainly a major dark-tea origin, but “birthplace” is a cultural title, not a single-point origin claim. The county’s tea factories and old tea paths are part of its heritage.

02 · Yangloudong, Hubei

Yangloudong is a historic tea town in the hills south of the Yangtze. It is associated with green brick tea and with the “洞茶” trade that supplied the nomadic markets. The old record calls it the “capital of Chinese brick tea” and says it had a hundred tea firms. The town’s old streets, tea warehouses and guild halls are real; the exact number of firms is a local estimate. Yangloudong is a useful place to see how brick tea was pressed and packed.

03 · Hankou

Hankou was the great tea port of the middle Yangtze. The old record calls it the end of the Han River boat route, where brick tea was transferred from boats to carts for the northward journey. Hankou’s tea trade in the nineteenth century was tied to the Yangtze network and to foreign export; the brick-tea trade to the northwest was a different branch. The city’s old concession area and riverside warehouses are part of that history.

04 · Xiangyang · Fancheng

Xiangyang’s Fancheng district sits on the Han River and was a trade hub between the Yangtze and the northwest. The old record says Shanxi and Shaanxi merchants gathered here, and that the Shan-Shaan and Fuzhou guild halls survive. Guild halls are a good marker of merchant networks: they provided lodging, credit, arbitration and rituals for traders from the same home region. The route’s tea trade would have used these networks, not a single company.

05 · Xi’an

Xi’an was the Tang capital and a long-standing northwest market. The old record says Chang’an had a busy tea market in the Tang and remained a tea-trade centre in the Ming and Qing. Xi’an’s role on the route was as a gateway between the Wei River valley and the Loess Plateau, not as a tea producer. The city’s Muslim Quarter and old bazaars are contemporary food markets, not reconstructions of the tea trade.

06 · Lanzhou

Lanzhou is the Yellow River crossing and the administrative centre of Gansu. The old record says tea merchants rested here and that the waterwheels along the river supplied the town. Lanzhou’s waterwheels are a real feature of the river landscape. The city was also the seat of the late Qing tea administration in the old record; that claim is treated as a plausible administrative history, not a verified office location.

07 · Wuwei

Wuwei, historically Liangzhou, is the eastern gateway of the Hexi Corridor. The old record says the Confucian temple has stele donated by tea merchants and that the Leitai Han tomb is near the old post road. The temple and the tomb are real heritage sites; the tea-merchant stele detail is a local memory. Wuwei’s oasis, springs and horse-breeding history made it a natural stop on the corridor.

08 · Zhangye

Zhangye is the route’s central market and the source of its name. The old record calls it “golden Zhangye” and says the Shanxi and Shaanxi guild halls and tea-merchant courtyards stood in the old city. Zhangye’s big Buddha temple, the wooden pagoda and the old guild halls are part of its urban heritage. The “largest tea market” and “tea-horse office” claims are local historical assertions; the city’s function as a redistribution point is the stronger, more general statement.

09 · Jiuquan

Jiuquan is the first major oasis west of Zhangye. The old record says its old street had shops and that tea merchants shared lodging with silk traders. Jiuquan’s name comes from a spring associated with a Han-dynasty general; the city was a garrison and a caravan stop. The tea trade here was part of a mixed cargo economy, not a tea-only route.

10 · Jiayuguan

Jiayuguan is the western end of the Ming Great Wall and the last pass before the Gobi. The old record says deep cart ruts remain at the pass and quotes the saying “out of Jiayuguan, tears never dry.” The pass and its fortifications are a major heritage site; the ruts and the saying are local memory. Beyond the pass, the route continued toward Dunhuang, Hami and Xinjiang, but those branches are outside the shared table.

Trade teas

The route is associated with Anhua dark brick tea, Yangloudong green brick tea, Ya’an Tibetan tea and Pu’er brick tea. These are broad categories of compressed tea. The old record’s list mixes producing regions and tea types; the page keeps them separate. Brick tea was valued in the northwest because it was dense, durable and easy to divide and boil.

Visiting note

The route is a historical corridor, not a preserved trail. Anhua, Yangloudong, Hankou, Xiangyang, Xi’an, Lanzhou, Wuwei, Zhangye, Jiuquan and Jiayuguan are reachable by rail or road. The Hexi Corridor is a dry, high-altitude region with strong seasonal temperature swings; check conditions before driving. The diagram is not a navigation aid. Tea factories and guild halls may be private or working sites; ask before entering.
