The Tang–Tubo Road

China

The Tang–Tubo Road linked Chang'an with Lhasa as an official corridor and a wider network of main, secondary and branch routes across the Tibetan Plateau. This article follows a thirty-station main-line reading sample and treats tea as a later Song–Ming–Qing trade story, not as a documented consequence of the 641 marriage mission.

Overview

The Tang–Tubo Road began at Chang'an, the Tang capital, and ended at Lhasa, the Tibetan capital of Logsar. It was not a single highway that appeared in 641. It was a working network: a main corridor, north–south secondary routes, branch valleys and passes, linked east to the Guanzhong plain, north to the Silk Roads and south to the Nepal road. Official accounts usually give a total length of more than 3,000 km, with about 1,630 km inside Qinghai. Xining marks the division between the eastern and western sections.

The road carried marriage missions, tribute and congratulatory embassies, Buddhist monks, soldiers, merchants and, in later centuries, tea traders. It has been called a "golden bridge" and the "southern Silk Road". Those are retrospective descriptions of its connecting role, not Tang-era official names. Treating it as one continuous walking trail flattens the way routes were reopened, diverted and recombined under different governments and different seasonal conditions.

History and background

A road before its name

People and goods moved between the Tibetan Plateau and the Central Plains long before the Tang. At the Karuo site in Qamdo, archaeologists found polished stone tools, jade, shells and millet with links to the Majiayao culture of the upper Yellow River. The material record points to early exchanges of products, techniques and people, not to a single road.

The Han and Sui governments maintained routes through the Hehuang region, the transitional zone between the Yellow River lowlands and the plateau. Han policy kept the Hehuang road open; in 609 the Sui emperor Yangdi campaigned against the Tuyuhun and strengthened the military corridor into Qinghai. These older routes gave the Tang–Tubo Road its physical foundation.

The name "Tang–Tubo Road" belongs to the period of Tang–Tibetan diplomacy. In 634 Songtsen Gampo sent his first mission to Chang'an. In 640 he sent the chief minister Gar Tongtsen with gold and gifts to request a marriage. In 641 Princess Wencheng travelled to Tibet under the escort of Li Daozong, the Prince of Jiangxia. From 634 to 850, Tibetan missions reached the Tang court 125 times and Tang missions went to Tibet 65 times. In 710 Princess Jincheng followed the same corridor. In 823 the two courts concluded the Changqing oath, whose bilingual tablet still stands in front of the Jokhang in Lhasa.

Those events gave a long-used highland corridor a clear political name and its busiest Tang-era phase. They did not freeze it into one line. Envoys, armies and caravans chose different valleys, passes and staging posts according to season, supply and political conditions. The line drawn on modern maps is an editorial reading sample assembled from texts and surviving sites.

The princesses and the limits of the tea story

Princess Wencheng's journey is the best-known event on the road. Official accounts describe her departure from Chang'an, her route through Gansu and Qinghai, the crossing of Riyue Mountain and Daheba, and the meeting with Songtsen Gampo at the Gyaring–Ngoring lakes, the ancient Baraing or Bohai lakes of the Yellow River source. The meeting is documented in the official narrative. The surrounding stories are not: the broken sun-and-moon mirror at Riyue Mountain, the river of homesick tears at Daotang, and the claim that the princess stayed for months in Yushu teaching farming, weaving and medicine are local legends or later retellings, not a day-by-day travel record.

Princess Jincheng travelled to Tibet in 710. Emperor Zhongzong held a farewell banquet at Shiping and the party continued west along the same corridor. The two marriages tightened political ties and increased traffic, but the princesses were not tea merchants. Tang sources do not describe a large tea dowry, and there is no contemporary record that identifies 641 as the moment tea entered Tibet or as the origin of the tea-horse trade. Those claims belong to later narratives that read the mature tea trade back into the seventh century.

Envoys, oaths and garrisons

In Tang times the road's core functions were official: embassies, marriage missions, troop movements, tribute and religious exchange. Chang'an was the starting point, Shancheng (modern Xining) the midpoint, Lhasa the terminus. Staging posts and garrison posts allowed envoys to change horses and replenish supplies with official documents. The relationship included war as well as oath-taking; the Changqing oath of 823 opened a comparatively stable phase of official exchange.

The corridor was also a Buddhist route. Princess Wencheng's party carried images and scriptures; Tang monks and envoys travelled through it toward India; Nepalese monks could come north through the Nepal road. The Vairocana Chapel at Bena Valley and the carved cliffs of Leba Valley in Yushu are surviving Tubo-period Buddhist sites from this traffic.

When tea entered the picture

The road's connection with tea belongs to a later layer of history. The National Ethnic Affairs Commission article states that after the Tang the road gradually became an important artery of the tea-horse road; the Yuan dynasty built the three postal systems of U-Tsang, Do-kham and Do-me on that foundation; and in the Ming and Qing the tea-horse trade became one of the most important contents of commercial exchange along the road. The Tibet Culture and Tourism Department interview makes a similar point: Northern Song tea-horse trade flourished along the route, and Ming–Qing tea-horse trade became central to the corridor's commercial life.

This is not the same as saying that Princess Wencheng brought tea in 641. Tea probably reached the plateau over a long period through gifts, tribute, religious offerings and merchant caravans. It became a mass daily drink only after border-tea production, brick-tea processing, transport organisation and market regulation developed in the Song, Ming and Qing. No single year can be identified as the switch. This article therefore writes "later overlap" and "Song–Ming–Qing expansion", not "the 641 origin".

Yushu: the Vairocana Chapel, not a princess statue

Yushu is one of the most important heritage areas on the Qinghai section. About 20 km southeast of Jiegu, at Bena Valley, stands a Tibetan-style chapel popularly called the Princess Wencheng Temple. Its proper name is the Vairocana Chapel. Inside, a cliff face carries a relief of one Buddha and eight bodhisattvas. Vairocana is the central figure, with eight bodhisattvas arranged in two tiers on either side. The images retain their Tubo-period form, although later generations added clay and colour. In 2006 the chapel and the Leba Valley carvings were listed together in the sixth batch of national key cultural relics.

There is no 500-metre statue of the princess here, and no eight-metre statue of Princess Wencheng. Claims of that kind confuse the central Buddha with a local commemorative figure and tourist storytelling. The popular name can be retained, but any account of the site must say what the main image is and who the figures represent.

Leba Valley lies more than 30 km east of Jiegu at about 3,538 m. Its cliffs carry Tubo-period Buddhas, stupas and old Tibetan inscriptions. The archaeological surveys of Bena and Leba provide material evidence for the date, route and Buddhist exchanges of the Tang–Tubo corridor.

Route and scope

The main-line reading sample

This article follows a main-line sample: Chang'an (Xi'an) → Xianyang → Mawei Post → Fengxiang → Dazhen Pass → Qinzhou (Tianshui) → Weizhou (Longxi) → Linzhou (Lintao) → Hezhou (Linxia) → Shanzhou (Ledu) → Xining → Huangyuan → Riyue Mountain → Daotang River → Qabqa (Princess Chapel) → Daheba → Huashixia → Maduo (Gyaring–Ngoring lakes) → Bayan Har Pass → Qingshuihe → Tongtian River crossing → Yushu (Jiegu) → Princess Wencheng Temple (Bena Valley) → Zadoi (Zi River) → Dangqu → Chawula Pass → Nierong → Nagqu → Damxung → Lhasa.

The station table totals about 2,850 km, with elevations from about 380 m to about 4,900 m. Official accounts give more than 3,000 km. The two figures use different start and end points, different route definitions and different methods of counting. They are kept side by side rather than averaged. About 1,630 km lie inside Qinghai, divided at Xining into eastern and western sections; the western section is the harder one. Official wording also differs between "four provinces" (Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai and Tibet) and "five provinces" (adding Sichuan). The difference reflects which approach routes are counted, not a simple error.

This is a reading sample, not the only route. The Qinghai Academy of Social Sciences researcher quoted by the Tibet Culture and Tourism Department describes the road as a network of a main line, two north–south secondary lines and many branches, connected south to the Nepal road and north to the Silk Roads. The old station table once drew Nangqian–Riwoqê–Qamdo–Dêngqên–Baqên–Sog–Nagqu as one continuous main line. That sequence is better treated as an eastern Tibetan branch. It belongs to the wider network, but its distances cannot be added directly to the Chang'an–Yushu–Chawula–Lhasa main line. The revised table removes it from the main route and explains it only in the network note.

Today G214 and G109 follow or parallel parts of the old corridor. The Qinghai Lake administration notes that G214 uses most of the historical route. Modern roads are transport and tourism corridors, not Tang postal roads, and they do not cover the Lhasa section described here.

Three distances, three scopes

The mileage problem is not a minor footnote. The 3,000-plus figure comes from official cultural-heritage and tourism accounts and describes the full Chang'an–Lhasa corridor. The 1,630 km figure describes only the Qinghai segment. The 2,850 km figure is the sum of the thirty stations in this table, rounded and measured along a simplified line. None of them is wrong in its own frame; mixing them produces a false precision. The diagram therefore labels its total as "station table" and keeps the official figure in the note.

Stations

The stations below use the same node_id as the route fact table. The map and elevation profile are reading aids, not a substitute for field data.

01 · Chang'an (Xi'an)

The Tang capital and the eastern terminus. Princess Wencheng left from here in 641, and later embassies, monks and caravans followed the same westward direction. The first stretch was an official capital road, not a mountain track.

02 · Xianyang Ancient Ferry

The Wei River crossing immediately west of Chang'an. Xianyang served as a gateway for east–west traffic in the Guanzhong plain; the modern riverbed and bridges have changed the old crossing's position.

03 · Mawei Post

A Tang staging post in the Xingping area. Mawei became famous through the 756 mutiny during the An Lushan rebellion, but the post existed before that event. Literary fame should not be confused with the postal system.

04 · Fengxiang

A political and military centre in western Guanzhong. Travelling parties replenished grain here before entering the Longshan mountains. The Zhou and Qin sites around Fengxiang show that the area had been a regional hub long before the Tang.

05 · Dazhen Pass (Longxian)

A key pass over the Longshan, the range that separates the Guanzhong plain from the Longyou highlands. The name is linked in local storytelling to an earthquake; the actual pass site and its road phases require archaeological evidence, not name etymology alone.

06 · Qinzhou (Tianshui)

The first major city west of Chang'an, about 310 km from the capital. The Maijishan grottoes lie in the western Qinling, in the zone where Silk Road and Tang–Tubo traffic overlapped. Qinzhou was a supply town, not the origin of the tea-horse trade.

07 · Weizhou (Longxi)

A loess-plateau town on the upper Wei River. West of here the landscape shifts from loess gullies to the Hehuang valley, with changes in farming, language and food.

08 · Linzhou (Lintao)

A border town on the Tao River. The Tao valley was one of the approaches from Longyou to Hezhou, although the exact postal line changed between dynasties. Lintao remained a frontier trade node from the Tang–Song period onward.

09 · Hezhou (Linxia)

A market town at the edge of the Tibetan Plateau and the upper Yellow River. Hezhou was important in Song, Ming and Qing tea-horse trade, and a tea-horse office was established in the Ming. It should not be called the single origin of the whole tea-horse system, nor projected back into the Tang.

10 · Shanzhou (Ledu)

An ancient town at the eastern end of the Huangshui valley and the seat of Tang Shanzhou. Entering here brings the traveller into the Qinghai section's agricultural belt of towns, monasteries and passes.

11 · Xining

Tang Shancheng, the point where the road divides into eastern and western sections. Xining was the main supply base: east to the Guanzhong and Longyou, west to Riyue Mountain, Qinghai Lake and the plateau.

12 · Huangyuan

The last river-valley town west of Xining, where the Qing dynasty established the Dangar sub-prefecture. Huangyuan was an important multi-ethnic trade gate, but titles such as "tea-horse capital" are later summaries, not Tang administrative facts.

13 · Riyue Mountain

The divide between the Huangshui valley and the Qinghai Lake basin, with a pass at about 3,200 m. The story of Princess Wencheng breaking her sun-and-moon mirror here is a later tale, not a Tang document. The mountain's role as an agricultural–pastoral boundary is better grounded than the legend.

14 · Daotang River

The Daotang flows northwest into Qinghai Lake, the opposite of most east-flowing rivers in the region. Local legend makes it the princess's homesick tears; geographically, the direction reflects the basin's structure and the lake's outlet history.

15 · Qabqa (Princess Chapel)

The Qabqa area of Gonghe County. Local tradition says the Tuyuhun built a residence for Princess Wencheng, hence the name "Princess Chapel". It is a commemorative narrative without a confirmed Tang-period site; it can be read as local memory, not as a verified residence.

16 · Daheba

A highland staging point between the Gonghe basin and the southern Qinghai plateau. The route turns southwest here, climbing through Huashixia toward the Yellow River source.

17 · Huashixia

A canyon town on the southern slope of the A'nyêmaqên range, an important supply point before the Yellow River source and the Bayan Har crossing. Today it is a highway town; the old track's exact alignment needs field evidence.

18 · Maduo (Gyaring–Ngoring Lakes)

Maduo County at the source of the Yellow River, where Gyaring and Ngoring lakes occupy the ancient Baraing/Bohai lake country. Official accounts place Songtsen Gampo's meeting with Princess Wencheng here. The meeting is documented; the exact ceremonial site and its reconstruction remain debated, so this article says "meeting", not "oath".

19 · Bayan Har Pass

The divide between the Yellow River and Yangtze source regions, with a station elevation of about 4,820 m. It is a major high pass, but it should not be called the road's absolute highest point without a route-wide survey; Chawula and the Tanggula crossings also matter.

20 · Qingshuihe

A high-grassland staging town in Chenduo County, Yushu Prefecture. It is the main transition east of the Tongtian River, at about 4,300 m; people and animals needed time to adjust before continuing.

21 · Tongtian River Crossing

A key crossing of the upper Yangtze. Historical travellers used skin rafts, wooden rafts and seasonal fords; modern bridges have replaced them. The name is not the same geographical concept as the Tongtian River of Journey to the West, and fiction should not be used as route evidence.

22 · Yushu (Jiegu)

Jiegu is a trade town at the meeting of Qinghai, Tibet and Sichuan, and a major hub on the Qinghai section. It sits at the gate of the Sanjiangyuan source region. The popular gloss "place where goods gather" is a local explanation; its etymology should be treated cautiously.

23 · Princess Wencheng Temple (Bena Valley)

The proper name is the Vairocana Chapel, about 20 km southeast of Jiegu in Bena Valley and more than 800 km from Xining; its western end connects to G214. It became a national key cultural relic in 2006. The cliff carries one Buddha and eight bodhisattvas, with Vairocana at the centre and the eight figures in two tiers. The images retain their Tubo-period form. There is no 500-metre princess statue and no eight-metre Princess Wencheng image here.

24 · Zadoi (Zi River)

Zadoi County in the Lancang River source region. The route follows the Zi River upstream into the Chawula mountains and the northern Tibetan plateau, where pastoral land replaces river-valley farming.

25 · Dangqu

The Dangqu basin in the Yangtze source region, at about 4,500 m. Alpine marsh and seasonal frozen ground made travel difficult; population and supplies are sparse, and this is one of the emptiest stretches of the route.

26 · Chawula Pass

The border pass between Zadoi in Qinghai and Nierong in Tibet, at about 4,900 m. It is a surviving main-line crossing from southern Qinghai into northern Tibet. It is not the Tanggula Pass on G109, the pass most modern tourists know by that name.

27 · Nierong

Nierong County in the upper Nu River pastoral country. The route continues toward Nagqu through grassland, wetland and low hills; winter snow can close the passes.

28 · Nagqu

The northern Tibetan town of Nagqu, historically called Heihe (Black River), at about 4,510 m. It was an important post and trade node on the western section, and is today a major stop on the Qinghai–Tibet highway and railway. The old post and the modern city occupy the same general area but are not the same settlement.

29 · Damxung

A pastoral county on the northern slope of the Nyainqêntanglha range, at about 4,285 m. It was the last major staging point before the Lhasa valley; caravans rested here, and today it is a stop on the Qinghai–Tibet road and railway.

30 · Lhasa

Logsar, the Tibetan capital and the western terminus. The Jokhang, Ramoche and the Changqing oath tablet of 823 are all in Lhasa. Tea became a daily plateau drink through long-distance trade and cultural exchange, not through a single marriage mission; calling Lhasa "the first stop of the tea Princess Wencheng brought" compresses a complex history into one story.

Trade teas

  • Ya'an dark tea (Nanlu border tea). A major brick tea of the Sichuan–Tibet trade, sold into Kham and Tibet. Its routes overlap the Tang–Tubo corridor, but its main direction is the Sichuan–Tibet tea road, not the official Chang'an–Lhasa axis.
  • Sichuan border tea. Including Kang brick and Jinjian, produced around Ya'an, Tianquan and Yingjing and carried through Kangding and Garzê into Tibetan areas. Border-tea trade became more institutionalised after the Song and expanded further in the Ming and Qing.
  • Pu'er. Tea from the Pu'er region of Yunnan, moved north along the Yunnan–Tibet routes and sold in Tibetan markets. It met the Tang–Tubo network in those markets; it did not travel the entire Chang'an–Lhasa line as a single cargo.
  • Fu brick tea. A brick tea traditionally processed and assembled around Jingyang in Shaanxi and circulated through northwest trade routes. Its "golden flowers" are the fungus Eurotium cristatum, a processing and storage feature; it should not be presented as the dominant Tang-era commodity of the road.

Mengding Ganlu is a famous Sichuan green tea and a tribute tea, not a main trade tea of the Tang–Tubo Road. The old page listed it among trade teas, which confused a celebrated garden tea with the border-tea trade. The Tang–Tubo Road gradually overlapped with the tea-horse trade after the Tang; the Song, Ming and Qing were the periods of institutionalisation and large-scale movement. No single reliable date marks the first arrival of tea in Tibet, and this article does not invent one.

Visiting notes

This page is a historical-geographical reading, not a hiking guide, a permit or a modern safety guarantee. Altitude, weather and road conditions are serious: the Bayan Har, Chawula, Nagqu and Damxung stretches can bring altitude sickness, blizzards and closures. Drivers and cyclists should follow current transport, weather and tourism notices and should not leave the road to follow the schematic line into remote areas.

The Vairocana Chapel and the Leba Valley carvings are national key cultural relics. Opening hours, tickets, photography and drone rules follow on-site notices. Do not climb images, touch murals or take rubbings; respect local religious custom. Parts of Tibet and border areas require permits under current regulations; check with local public-security and tourism offices before travelling.
