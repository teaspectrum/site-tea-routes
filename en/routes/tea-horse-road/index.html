The Ancient Tea Horse Road

China

The Ancient Tea Horse Road runs from Pu’er (Ning’er) to Lhasa, with 42 stations. Read the endpoints and station table first, then ask which producing areas, markets and transport links it connected; it is not modern navigation.

Overview

The Ancient Tea Horse Road is not a single road but a network of trade routes linking the tea-producing regions of southwest China with the Tibetan plateau and, through the Himalayan passes, with South Asia. It is associated with the exchange of tea for horses and with mule and horse caravans that crossed some of the highest and most difficult terrain in Asia. The routes also carried salt, cloth, medicinal goods and ideas, and connected Han, Tibetan, Dai, Yi, Naxi and other communities.

History and background

Tang period (618–907): the beginnings of tea-horse exchange. In 641, Princess Wencheng is recorded as travelling to Tibet to marry the Tibetan ruler Songtsen Gampo with tea among her goods. Over the following century, tea became increasingly valued on the plateau as a drink that helped balance a diet rich in meat and butter. Tibet could not grow tea, so highland horses were exchanged for tea from Yunnan and Sichuan. In 864, the local record Man Shu noted tea growing in the mountains around Yinsheng, an early written reference to Yunnan tea.

Song period (960–1279): the tea-horse office. Facing military pressure from northern states, the Song court needed horses and formalised the exchange. In 1074, a tea-horse office was established in Ya'an, Sichuan, to manage the official trade in Sichuan tea for Tibetan horses. Sichuan border tea was compressed into bricks and carried by porters and caravans over the Erlang and Zheduo mountains to Kangding, where it was traded with Tibetan merchants. The corridor thus moved from local exchange to a strategic state institution.

Yuan period (1271–1368): the postal network. After the Mongol conquest of Tibet, the Yuan state built a system of relay stations — zhanchi — along the existing routes, roughly every 30 to 60 li, with horses, food and lodging for officials and envoys. The conquest reduced the autonomy of local polities along the Hengduan range and opened the corridor to regular official traffic. Yunnan tea, especially Pu'er, began to move into Tibet in larger volume; the journey was longer than the Sichuan route, but the tea was valued by monasteries and nobles.

Ming period (1368–1644): tea permits and official control. The Ming continued the tea-horse market system and required merchants to carry government tea permits. Tea-horse offices were set up in Taozhou, Hezhou, Xining and other places. The name "Pu tea" appears in Xie Zhaozhe's Dianlue (1620), an early record of Pu'er tea. Official policy treated tea as a means of managing relations with Tibetan regions, while private trade continued alongside it.

Qing period (1644–1912): the caravan economy at its height. In 1729, the Pu'er prefecture was established and Pu'er tea entered the tribute system. The Pu'er Prefecture Gazetteer records that Pu'er tea was known throughout the empire and especially valued in the capital. In the Qing period the Yunnan–Tibet trade expanded: thousands of muleteers and tens of thousands of animals moved between the gorges and passes, and annual tea shipments were counted in the tens of thousands of dan. A caravan might contain fifty to a hundred mules, each carrying sixty to eighty kilograms; the caravan leader, the maguotou, needed knowledge of routes, weather, animal care and local negotiations.

The 1939 Dianhong story. The tea gardens around Fengqing, with their old large-leaf trees, already existed in the caravan era, but their leaf was then carried as sun-dried raw material for Pu'er. In 1939, with the southeast tea districts lost to war, Feng Shaoqiu was sent to Yunnan to find a new black-tea base. He travelled into Fengqing along the old road and made the first Dianhong from local large-leaf material; the tea caused a sensation at the London market. The end of the caravan road thus became the starting point for a Chinese black tea sold to the world.

A network, not one line. The main Yunnan–Tibet corridor ran from the Pu'er region through Dali, Lijiang and Shangri-La, crossed the Hengduan mountains and the upper Yangtze, Mekong and Salween gorges, and reached Lhasa. A Sichuan branch connected Ya'an and the Chengdu plain with the plateau. Goods moved in stages, with local traders, inns, ferries and pastures along the way. The routes continued beyond Lhasa toward Nepal, Bhutan and India, so the "end" of the Yunnan–Tibet road was also a point of onward trade.

Republic to the present: decline and reinvention. In the 1930s the Burma Road and the Sichuan–Kangding road reached the region, and motor vehicles gradually replaced the caravans. In the 1950s the G318 highway covered much of the Sichuan–Tibet section. The old route fell quiet, but its tea economy did not disappear. In 1973 the Menghai tea factory developed artificial pile fermentation, creating ripe Pu'er; after 2000, famous old-tree gardens such as Laobanzhang and Bingdao revived; in 2013 the Tea Horse Road was listed as a national key cultural relic. Today Lashihai, Dukezong and Feilai Temple are places where visitors can still read a fragment of the old road.

Route and scope

The migrated record describes a main Yunnan–Tibet route of about 2,250 kilometres, from Pu'er (modern Ning'er) through Dali, Lijiang, Zhongdian (Shangri-La), Deqin, Yanjing, Mangkam and on to Lhasa. The shared station table lists 42 nodes, from Pu'er at about 1,320 metres to the Meili pass at about 4,820 metres; across the whole journey the elevation runs from roughly 500 metres in the tropical lowlands to over 5,000 metres at the high passes, before settling at Lhasa’s 3,650 metres. The route crosses seven passes above 4,000 metres and the Jinsha, Lancang and Nujiang gorges. The old record gives three to four months one way and six to eight months for a return journey, with a recommended season from mid-May to mid-October.

These distances, durations and elevations are historical route data. They are not a modern survey, a GPS track or a walking itinerary. Some sections are now roads; others are protected heritage paths, farmland, village lanes or inaccessible terrain. The shared station table is the single source for node names, coordinates and elevations used by the map and elevation profile on this page.

Stations

The stations below use the same node_id as the route fact table. The map and elevation profile are reading aids, not a substitute for field data.

01 · Pu’er (Ning’er)

Cumulative distance about 0 km; elevation about 1,320 m; coordinates 23.07, 101.05. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The starting point and the seat of the old Pu'er prefecture, the source of the name Pu'er tea. The zero-kilometre stone for the Tea Horse Road stands in the old town, and the flagstones of the ancient post station are worn into deep ruts by horses’ hooves.

02 · Mohei Old Town

Cumulative distance about 22 km; elevation about 1,280 m; coordinates 23.18, 101.1. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The first town north of Pu'er and a centre of well salt. Caravans loaded salt and tea together here — tea and salt were never separated on this road.

03 · Babian River Ferry

Cumulative distance about 45 km; elevation about 1,150 m; coordinates 23.3, 101.15. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The first great river crossing on the route. In the dry season the caravan forded the Babian River; in the rains it used a rattan suspension bridge, sending people and animals across separately — a hair-raising business.

04 · Zhenyuan Enle Basin

Cumulative distance about 68 km; elevation about 1,300 m; coordinates 23.92, 101.07. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A rich basin at the foot of the Ailao mountains. After the crossing, caravans restocked with glutinous rice cakes and bananas at Enle for the climb ahead.

05 · Jinggu Dai and Yi Township

Cumulative distance about 95 km; elevation about 1,400 m; coordinates 23.5, 100.7. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

An important source of leaf for old Pu'er teas. The Dai villages here still keep the custom of roasting tea in bamboo tubes; caravans replenished fodder and dry food.

06 · Qianjiazhai Entrance

Cumulative distance about 130 km; elevation about 1,320 m; coordinates 24, 101.2. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The edge of the Ailao primeval forest. From here the route enters dense woodland — “three days in the forest without seeing the sun” — with tigers, snakes and other hazards to guard against.

07 · Qianjiazhai

Cumulative distance about 160 km; elevation about 1,250 m; coordinates 24.1, 101.25. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

An old tea mountain in the heart of Ailao and the home of a wild tea tree said to be 2,700 years old. Caravans spent days in the forest with only birds and animals for company.

08 · Ailao Mountain Pass

Cumulative distance about 210 km; elevation about 1,550 m; coordinates 24.3, 101.3. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The highest point of the Ailao crossing. The loose stone slope is treacherous; caravans unloaded part of the cargo, sent the empty animals ahead, then came back for a second load.

09 · Jingdong Wuliang Mountain

Cumulative distance about 260 km; elevation about 1,700 m; coordinates 24.45, 100.85. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

Jingdong’s Wuliang Mountain, the cloud-and-mist tea country made famous in Jin Yong’s novels. Every year animals slipped off the scree; the caravans called this stretch the “horse-losing slope.”

10 · Nanjian Lingbao Mountain

Cumulative distance about 320 km; elevation about 1,850 m; coordinates 24.85, 100.55. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A gorge corridor between the Wuliang and Ailao ranges. The old stone temple on Lingbao Mountain was where caravans prayed for safe passage — “pray at Lingbao, and Dali is before you.”

11 · Dali Old Town

Cumulative distance about 380 km; elevation about 2,050 m; coordinates 25.6, 100.23. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The old capital of the Nanzhao and Dali kingdoms and the largest caravan market on the road. Thousands of mules gathered here in spring and autumn; the March Street tea-horse market has run for centuries.

12 · Eryuan Dengchuan Basin

Cumulative distance about 430 km; elevation about 2,200 m; coordinates 26, 100.05. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A fertile Bai basin known for Dengchuan milk fan and Eryuan plums. Caravans bought milk fan and preserved plums here as a source of vitamins for the high plateau.

13 · Jianchuan Shaxi Old Town

Cumulative distance about 480 km; elevation about 2,350 m; coordinates 26.33, 99.85. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

Shaxi is the only surviving old market town on the Tea Horse Road. Sideng Street still has the hitching stones for caravans and century-old inns.

14 · Jiuhe Bai Township

Cumulative distance about 520 km; elevation about 2,380 m; coordinates 26.55, 100. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The southern gate of Lijiang and a borderland where Bai and Naxi communities meet. Caravans took the Bai three-course tea here for the last time; beyond Lijiang they switched to the Naxi “dragon and tiger” tea.

15 · Lijiang Old Town

Cumulative distance about 560 km; elevation about 2,410 m; coordinates 26.87, 100.23. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The Naxi caravan hub, where the flagstones still show deep hoof marks. Sifang Street was the unloading square and once the busiest transshipment point on the road.

16 · Lashihai Wetland

Cumulative distance about 600 km; elevation about 2,700 m; coordinates 26.88, 100.15. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A highland wetland pasture where caravans rested and grazed their animals. A stretch of the old Tea Horse Road can still be ridden around Lashihai.

17 · Tiger Leaping Gorge

Cumulative distance about 640 km; elevation about 3,100 m; coordinates 27.15, 100.03. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

One of the deepest gorges in the world. The old path was cut into the cliff, barely wide enough for one horse, with the Jinsha River roaring 600 metres below; caravans called it the “life-abandoning gorge.”

18 · Xiaozhongdian Grassland

Cumulative distance about 690 km; elevation about 3,300 m; coordinates 27.45, 99.8. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The high meadow at the southern gate of Shangri-La. In midsummer the grassland is a sea of flowers; caravans camped among the gentians and Tibetan herders brought fresh butter tea.

19 · Zhongdian (Shangri-La)

Cumulative distance about 740 km; elevation about 3,310 m; coordinates 27.82, 99.7. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The gateway town of the Tibetan region. Dukezong old town is about 1,300 years old and was an important tea-horse market; the hilltop park has a giant prayer wheel.

20 · Nixi Benzilan

Cumulative distance about 790 km; elevation about 3,500 m; coordinates 28.12, 99.45. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A dry-hot valley village at the great bend of the Jinsha River. The path follows the cliff above the water — “you can see the river but cannot drink from it.”

21 · Dongzhulin Pass

Cumulative distance about 830 km; elevation about 3,800 m; coordinates 28.35, 99.3. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The outpost before the Baima Snow Mountain country. In early summer the snowmelt leaves the road muddy and animals sink to the fetlock; caravans laid planks to get through.

22 · Baima Snow Mountain Pass

Cumulative distance about 850 km; elevation about 3,900 m; coordinates 28.4, 99.1. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A pass at about 3,900 metres. Snow can linger in summer and the air is thin; caravans sucked lumps of brown sugar for energy and stopped every hundred paces to breathe.

23 · Deqin Feilai Temple

Cumulative distance about 920 km; elevation about 3,450 m; coordinates 28.48, 98.88. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The supply station before the Meili Snow Mountain crossing. From here caravans looked up at Kawakarpo (6,740 m), offered white scarves and burned juniper to ask the mountain’s protection.

24 · Meili North Slope Camp

Cumulative distance about 960 km; elevation about 4,300 m; coordinates 28.5, 98.78. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The last camp before the Meili pass, above 4,000 metres. The animals breathed hard; caravans rested half the night and set off before dawn for the summit.

25 · Meili Snow Mountain Pass

Cumulative distance about 980 km; elevation about 4,820 m; coordinates 28.55, 98.7. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The highest and most dangerous point on the whole route, at 4,820 metres. Caravans left at three in the morning and had to cross before noon — “one step late and you stay on the mountain forever.” Dozens of caravans are said to lie here.

26 · Foshan Qucika

Cumulative distance about 1,020 km; elevation about 3,400 m; coordinates 28.6, 98.65. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A hot-spring village in the Lancang gorge. After the pass, caravans soaked away the cold; “Qucika” means “hot spring” in Tibetan, and it was the happiest rest stop on the road.

27 · Yanjing (Tibet)

Cumulative distance about 1,060 km; elevation about 2,650 m; coordinates 28.85, 98.58. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The first stop after the Yunnan–Tibet border, with ancient salt pans that shine like mirrors. Caravans took on salt and tsampa; tea and Tibetan salt were traded here — “past Yanjing there is no rice.”

28 · Qucika Gorge

Cumulative distance about 1,120 km; elevation about 3,300 m; coordinates 28.9, 98.55. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

A deep cut of the Lancang River, with cliffs falling straight to the water. The path narrows to half a metre, the river roaring outside and smooth rock inside.

29 · Mangkam

Cumulative distance about 1,180 km; elevation about 3,850 m; coordinates 29.68, 98.6. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The junction of the Yunnan–Tibet and Sichuan–Tibet routes and the gateway to eastern Tibet. The two Tea Horse roads merge here, and the caravans double in size; hot springs wash off the dust.

30 · Zogang Dongda Mountain

Cumulative distance about 1,280 km; elevation about 4,100 m; coordinates 29.67, 97.85. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The Dongda Mountain pass, at 5,008 metres, has thin air. Caravans could not linger; “no birds fly on Dongda, only hooves on snow.”

31 · Tiantuo Yuqu Valley

Cumulative distance about 1,380 km; elevation about 4,250 m; coordinates 30.08, 97.55. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

Grassland in the upper Yuqu valley, a tributary of the Nujiang. In summer the valley is full of wildflowers; caravans grazed for two days to recover before the Bangda highland.

32 · Bangda Grassland

Cumulative distance about 1,480 km; elevation about 4,350 m; coordinates 30.4, 97.3. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

High cold grassland at about 4,300 metres. Tibetan families received caravans in black yak-hair tents — “Bangda’s butter tea warms the heart” — and a rest of several days was usual.

33 · Nujiang 72 Turns Summit

Cumulative distance about 1,550 km; elevation about 4,100 m; coordinates 30.2, 97.1. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The top of Yela Mountain, looking down on the seventy-two turns coiled like a great snake. The descent took a whole day, with scree sliding underfoot through every bend.

34 · Luolong Nujiang Gorge

Cumulative distance about 1,620 km; elevation about 3,800 m; coordinates 30.12, 96.8. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

Deep in the Nujiang gorge, where the old path hangs on the cliff and narrows to less than a metre beside a sheer drop. Caravans spoke in low voices, afraid to disturb the mountain spirits.

35 · Exi Nyainqentanglha Foothills

Cumulative distance about 1,700 km; elevation about 4,000 m; coordinates 30.5, 95.5. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The eastern extension of the Nyainqentanglha range. The slope eases but the altitude does not; the animals’ shoes wore out fastest here and had to be replaced.

36 · Bianba Three-Color Lakes

Cumulative distance about 1,780 km; elevation about 4,200 m; coordinates 30.93, 94.7. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The three-coloured lakes of Bianba — black, white and yellow — a hidden corner of northeastern Tibet. Caravans took fresh water here and, by local custom, threw stones to ask the lakes about the road ahead.

37 · Biru Sapu Mountain

Cumulative distance about 1,880 km; elevation about 3,900 m; coordinates 31.48, 93.68. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

Below Sapu Mountain (6,956 m), a near-perfect triangular snow peak. Caravans worshipped the mountain; seeing its full face was said to bring a safe journey.

38 · Gongbo’gyamda Basum Lake

Cumulative distance about 1,980 km; elevation about 3,700 m; coordinates 29.88, 93.25. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

Basum Lake, the “emerald of the plateau,” surrounded by pine forest. This is the last green corridor before Lhasa; the animals recovered and the caravan sang through the woods.

39 · Songduo Hot Spring

Cumulative distance about 2,040 km; elevation about 3,900 m; coordinates 29.92, 92.5. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The last Tibetan hot-spring stop below the Mira pass. Caravans unloaded and bathed — “one soak at Songduo and Lhasa is carefree” — washing off two thousand kilometres of dust.

40 · Mira Mountain Pass

Cumulative distance about 2,100 km; elevation about 4,100 m; coordinates 29.85, 92.3. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The final pass, at 5,013 metres. Beyond it the Lhasa valley opens and prayer flags snap in the wind: “see Mira Mountain and Lhasa is before you.”

41 · Maizhokunggar Gyama Valley

Cumulative distance about 2,170 km; elevation about 3,800 m; coordinates 29.83, 91.73. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The birthplace of Songtsen Gampo, where the Lhasa valley suddenly opens. “When the rapeseed blooms at Gyama, the caravan knows home is near.” Tea was repacked here for the last stage.

42 · Lhasa

Cumulative distance about 2,250 km; elevation about 3,650 m; coordinates 29.65, 91.12. Data context: migrated historical route data, not a modern navigation track.

The terminus, the holy city of the snow land. Butter-tea fragrance and juniper smoke mingle around the Barkhor and Jokhang; Tibetans traded good horses for Pu'er bricks — “the Tea Horse Road ends here, complete.”

Trade teas

Pu'er tea is the best-known tea of the route. Raw Pu'er, ripe Pu'er, Fengqing black tea and Ya'an brick tea are recorded in the old data, together with Mengding Ganlu and Sichuan brick tea. A trade association is a historical relationship, not proof that every stop produced the same tea. The rise of ripe Pu'er in the twentieth century, for example, belongs to a later industrial period than the caravan trade described above.

Visiting note

The historical route can be read through museums, old markets, tea shops and heritage sites in Pu'er, Dali, Lijiang, Shaxi, Shangri-La and Deqin. This site does not provide turn-by-turn navigation, current road conditions or a promise that the historic route can be walked. Access, weather, altitude and local rules must be checked independently.

Raw Pu-erh

Raw Pu-erh (sheng pu'er) is one of the two product types of geographical-indication Pu-erh: Yunnan large-leaf sun-dried tea, steamed, pressed and dried, without pile fermentation. It starts bright, floral and bitter, then changes with storage — but ageing is a storage outcome, not a standard guarantee.

陆羽（约 733–804）是唐代茶学家，著《茶经》，被后世尊为“茶圣”。他把茶的源流、采制、器具、煮饮、历史和产地系统化，设计二十四器，提出“山水上，江水中，井水下”等用水原则；他不是“发明茶的人”，而是把茶从地方经验整理成文化体系的人。

Fengqing Dianhong

Fengqing Dianhong is a congou black tea from Fengqing, Yunnan, made from large-leaf cultivars. It has plump, downy leaves, a high aroma and a strong, full taste. Check origin, grade and standard on the label; Dianhong is not Pu-erh.
