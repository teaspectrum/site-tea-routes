China

Overview

The Tibet–Nepal tea road, known in Chinese as 蕃尼古道, is a high-altitude trade corridor between Lhasa and the Kathmandu Valley. The shared table records ten nodes over about 880 kilometres: Lhasa, Yamdrok Lake, Nagarzê, Gyantse, Shigatse, Lhatse, Tingri, Nyalam, Zhangmu and Kathmandu. The old Chinese record gives a longer figure of about 1,200 kilometres; the difference reflects different route variants and the difficulty of measuring a caravan road across the Himalaya.

The corridor is usually discussed through tea, but it carried much more. Tibetan brick tea, Sichuan and Yunnan tea, wool, salt, hides, herbs, textiles and metalware moved in both directions. The route is also a cultural corridor: monasteries, fortresses and market towns are strung along it, and the Kathmandu Valley’s heritage is part of the same exchange system.

History and background

Trade between the Tibetan Plateau and the Kathmandu Valley is long established. The old record places the route’s origins in the seventh century and names Songtsen Gampo and Princess Wencheng. Those names belong to the founding narrative of the Tibetan empire and the China–Tibet relationship; they are historically important, but the precise date of 639 for the first tea carriage is a traditional account, not a fact that this page can verify.

The route’s modern shape was shaped by the tea trade and by the political geography of the Himalaya. Lhasa was the administrative and religious centre; Shigatse was the seat of the Panchen Lama and a major market; Gyantse was a fortress town; Tingri and Nyalam were highland stages; Zhangmu was the border landing on the Nepalese side. Kathmandu was the terminus and the point where Tibetan goods entered the South Asian market.

Transport was by pack animal and human porterage. Yaks and mules carried loads over passes; in the deep gorge below Nyalam, porters and mule trains took over. The old record’s claim that the route was “the highest tea road in the world” is a superlative that depends on how one defines a tea road; this article avoids it. What can be said is that the route crosses some of the highest inhabited terrain on earth, with stations between about 1,400 and 4,441 metres.

The 2015 Nepal earthquake damaged heritage buildings in the Kathmandu Valley and affected settlements along the border. Restoration has been uneven. Travellers should check the current condition of monuments and border crossings rather than rely on pre-2015 descriptions.

Route and scope

The ten nodes run from Lhasa (0 km, about 3,650 m) through Yamdrok Lake (100 km, about 4,441 m), Nagarzê (180 km, about 4,040 m), Gyantse (260 km, about 4,040 m), Shigatse (360 km, about 3,836 m), Lhatse (510 km, about 4,010 m), Tingri (600 km, about 4,300 m), Nyalam (720 km, about 3,750 m), Zhangmu (760 km, about 2,300 m) and Kathmandu (880 km, about 1,400 m). The route is a historical caravan corridor, not a trekking itinerary. Several sections require permits, and the high passes are seasonal.

Stations

The stations below use the same node_id as the route fact table. The map and elevation profile are reading aids, not a substitute for field data.

01 · Lhasa

Lhasa is the starting point. The old record describes the Barkhor, the circuit around the Jokhang, as a tea distribution centre where Sichuan and Yunnan merchants handed tea to Tibetan–Nepalese traders. The Barkhor is still a commercial and pilgrimage space, but the contemporary tea trade is more dispersed. Lhasa’s role in the route was as a political, religious and market hub, not as a tea-growing area.

02 · Yamdrok Lake

Yamdrok Lake, one of the three great sacred lakes of Tibet, lies at about 4,441 metres. The old record describes a lakeside post where tea caravans watered and rested. The lake’s colour changes with light and weather; the “turquoise of the goddess” image is a cultural description, not a scientific one. The pass above the lake is a high point of the route and a place where altitude illness is a real risk.

03 · Nagarzê

Nagarzê is a small town on the western shore of Yamdrok Lake, below the Noijin Kangsang range, whose highest peak reaches 7,191 metres. The old record calls it a post where caravans changed horses. The surrounding high pastures and glacial streams are part of the route’s water and fodder economy. The town is administratively in Shannan prefecture; travellers should not treat the old station list as a modern itinerary.

04 · Gyantse

Gyantse is a fortress town on the Nyang River. The Gyantse Dzong and the Pelkhor Chode monastery, with its Kumbum stupa, are the town’s best-known monuments. The old record calls Gyantse a key pass on the Tibet–Nepal route and says Nepalese traders valued Gyantse carpets. Carpet production is a real local craft, but the claim about Nepalese demand is a trade anecdote rather than a documented statistic.

05 · Shigatse

Shigatse is the second city of Tibet and the seat of the Panchen Lama at Tashilhunpo monastery. The old record describes it as the largest redistribution market on the route: Yunnan brick tea and Sichuan Fu brick were repacked and loaded onto yak caravans. That function is plausible for a major monastic and administrative centre. The modern city is a rail and road hub, and the old caravan economy has largely been replaced.

06 · Lhatse

Lhatse is a highland town at the junction of routes toward Tingri and the Everest region. The old record calls it the last supply point before the Himalayan passes and mentions Lhatse knives. Lhatse is indeed known for metalwork, but the “knife for self-defence” framing is a traveller’s story. The practical point is that after Lhatse, supplies, shelter and oxygen become more limited.

07 · Tingri

Tingri is the northern gateway to the Everest region. The old record describes the old town as a stage where caravans could see Everest and says “when you see Everest, the road is near.” That is a saying, not a distance measurement. Tingri is high, windy and cold; the modern road and the old caravan track are not the same thing.

08 · Nyalam

Nyalam, meaning “elephant’s neck,” sits in a deep gorge on the southern slope of the Himalaya. The old record describes a rapid descent from the plateau into forest, with a change in vegetation and humidity. That contrast is one of the route’s defining experiences. The road from Nyalam to Zhangmu is steep and landslide-prone; travellers should check conditions.

09 · Zhangmu

Zhangmu is the border town on the Chinese side, at about 2,300 metres. The old record gives the descent from around 5,000 metres to 2,300 metres, a drop of nearly 3,000 metres, and calls it a deep gorge where caravans changed to Nepalese porters. The town was severely affected by the 2015 earthquake and by later border and road changes; its role as a busy transit point has fluctuated. The old description should not be used as a current travel guide.

10 · Kathmandu

Kathmandu is the terminus. The city’s history long predates 723 CE, and the old record’s foundation date of 723 should not be treated as a founding year. The old record names the Thamel and Asan areas as tea trading centres, with Tibetan brick tea repacked for Indian markets and Nepalese herbs, spices and copperware returning north. Thamel is now a tourist district; Asan is still a market. The Kathmandu Valley’s UNESCO-listed monuments are part of the route’s cultural context, but tea is only one strand of the exchange.

Trade teas

The route is associated with Tibetan brick tea, Sichuan Biancha, Fu brick tea, Pu’er and Anhua Tianjian. These categories overlap. “Tibetan brick tea” is a trade form — compressed tea for transport and for boiling with butter and salt — not a single cultivar. The old record’s tea list should be read as a cargo mix.

Visiting note

This is a high-altitude route with international border crossings. Do not attempt it without acclimatisation, permits and current information. The diagram is schematic, and the station elevations are historical approximations. Zhangmu’s border status and the condition of roads on the Nepalese side should be checked locally.

Sources and status

This English article is a full English edition of the Chinese route record, checked against the Nepal Tourism Board, the Tibet Autonomous Region government portal and UNESCO’s Kathmandu Valley listing. It is marked reviewed for bilingual source-check and claim-boundary purposes on 2026-11-22. China · Yunnan

Raw Pu-erh

Raw Pu-erh (sheng pu'er) is one of the two product types of geographical-indication Pu-erh: Yunnan large-leaf sun-dried tea, steamed, pressed and dried, without pile fermentation. It starts bright, floral and bitter, then changes with storage — but ageing is a storage outcome, not a standard guarantee.

China · Shaanxi

Shaanxi Fu Brick Tea

Shaanxi Fu brick tea is a post-fermented dark tea centred on Jingyang in Xianyang, Shaanxi. It is made from black maocha from southern Shaanxi, Hunan and Sichuan, then pile-fermented, steamed, pressed and incubated so that golden “flowers” of Eurotium cristatum grow inside the brick. Jingyang Fu brick tea received geographical indication protection in 2013; Xianyang Fu tea making was added to the national intangible heritage list in 2021. It is not a black tea, and golden flowers are not evidence of a health effect.
