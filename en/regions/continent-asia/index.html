This page lists 18 routes: The Ancient Tea Horse Road, The Ancient Tea Road of Southwest China, The Arabian Tea Route, The Darjeeling Himalayan Railway. This place page is an index; route details, stations and source boundaries live on the route pages.

18entries

This page groups routes by continent and only answers “where to start reading”. Routes are not modern navigation: node coordinates, distances and elevations explain trade and geography, not walking or driving directions.

Reading order: start with the route summary, then the station table, then the sources and limits. Routes differ in goods, period and transport; one generic “tea road” frame cannot explain them all.

Asia

The Southern Silk Road Tea Route

The Southern Silk Road is a multi-era network from the Chengdu plain through Sichuan and Yunnan into Myanmar and northeast India, not a single ancient highway and not a route that carried tea from end to end. This article follows a 32-station reading line from Chengdu through Ya'an, Xichang, Dali, Baoshan, Tengchong, Bhamo, Myitkyina, the Pangsau Pass and the Assam valley to Guwahati, separating the Han-era Shu-Shendu corridor, later tea-horse trade, Qing and Republican Pu'er exports, the nineteenth-century Assam plantation industry and the twentieth-century Stilwell Road. It rejects the claims of a fourth-century-BCE tea road, direct Yunnan-to-Assam tea-seed transmission and an Ahom origin for Assamese tea drinking.

The Tang–Tubo Road

The Tang–Tubo Road linked Chang'an with Lhasa as an official corridor and a wider network of main, secondary and branch routes across the Tibetan Plateau. This article follows a thirty-station main-line reading sample and treats tea as a later Song–Ming–Qing trade story, not as a documented consequence of the 641 marriage mission.

The Yunnan–Burma–India Tea Road

The Yunnan–Burma–India Tea Road is a thematic corridor from Dali through Baoshan and Tengchong into Myanmar, over the Patkai Pass, and down the Brahmaputra valley to Guwahati. This article separates the movement of Yunnan tea, Burmese pickled tea, the nineteenth-century Assam plantation industry and the Tai-Ahom migration, and refuses a single-line “Yunnan tea seed to Assam” origin story.
